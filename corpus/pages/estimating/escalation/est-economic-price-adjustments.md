## Purpose

Shows what an auditor checks when a proposal relies on an economic price adjustment: the method, the accounting ability to isolate economic changes, the double-counting rule, and the ceiling stated in the audit manual. The clause rules are on the clause cards.

An economic price adjustment is a contractual pricing mechanism used during periods of economic uncertainty or extended performance to protect both the Government and the contractor from abnormal, uncontrollable wage and material price fluctuations [@CL-EST-0729]. The audit manual describes three methods. Established prices apply to basic commodities and standard commercial supplies based on published catalog or market rates [@CL-EST-0730]. Actual costs apply to contracts lacking major design or research and development, where adjustments track verified labor and material cost changes experienced during performance [@CL-EST-0731]. Cost indexes link adjustments to recognized external benchmarks, such as Bureau of Labor Statistics labor and material indices [@CL-EST-0732].

This card gives the auditor's view. The clause rules for standard supplies, semistandard supplies, and labor and material are on [Economic Price Adjustment, Standard Supplies clause](est-epa-standard-supplies.md), [Economic Price Adjustment, Semistandard Supplies clause](est-epa-semistandard-supplies.md), and [Economic Price Adjustment, Labor and Material](../../contract-finance/billing-payment/ctf-economic-price-adjustment-labor-and-material.md).

## How it works

A contractor using these provisions must ensure that its accounting systems can clearly identify, segregate, and track specific economic cost changes apart from qualitative, quantitative, or scope-related variances [@CL-EST-0851]. The baseline contract price and the adjustment clause must share the exact same base period, and cost factors must not be double counted, so inflation factors are not backed into the base proposal while also claimed under the clause [@CL-EST-0733]. Adjustments are restricted solely to market-driven changes beyond the contractor's control [@CL-EST-0850]. For the established-price and actual-cost methods, the audit manual states that aggregate price increases are capped at a 10 percent ceiling above the original contract unit price unless formally modified by the Chief of the Contracting Office [@CL-EST-0734].

## Worked example

Synthetic example. The numbers are made up.

A proposal prices a unit at 100 using an actual-cost method. The base price must not carry an inflation factor that the clause also pays, so the contractor leaves any escalation out of the base and claims market-driven cost movement only through the clause. In aggregate the unit price can rise to at most 110 unless the Chief of the Contracting Office formally modifies the ceiling.

## Never confuse

An economic price adjustment is not a scope change: it is restricted to market-driven changes beyond the contractor's control, and economic cost changes must be tracked apart from scope-related variances [@CL-EST-0850; @CL-EST-0851]. The ceiling is stated for the established-price and actual-cost methods [@CL-EST-0734].

## Company calibration

That escalation factors are not double counted and that the base period matches the contract. [@Q-023]
