## Purpose

Shows how much risk a firm-fixed-price contract leaves with the contractor and when the FAR treats the type as suitable.

## The rule

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Instrument | Codified FAR [@CL-EST-1150] |
| Codified citation | FAR 16.202-1 and FAR 16.202-2 [@CL-EST-1150; @CL-EST-1151] |
| RFO citation | Not established. |
| Edition date | Not established. |
| Text status | Final [@CL-EST-1150] |
| Applies to | Contracts of the firm-fixed-price type [@CL-EST-1150] |

A firm-fixed-price contract has a price that is not subject to any adjustment on the basis of the contractor's cost experience, and the contract type places maximum risk and full responsibility for all costs and resulting profit or loss on the contractor [@CL-EST-1150].

The FAR names commercial products or commercial services, and other supplies or services bought on reasonably definite functional or detailed specifications, as the things the type is suitable for acquiring, when the contracting officer can establish fair and reasonable prices at the outset [@CL-EST-1151; @CL-EST-1152].

## How it works

The text ties suitability to the contracting officer's ability to establish fair and reasonable prices at the outset, and it gives examples introduced by the words "such as" rather than a closed list [@CL-EST-1152]. The first example is adequate price competition [@CL-EST-1152]. The next two are reasonable price comparisons with prior purchases of the same or similar supplies or services made on a competitive basis or supported by valid certified cost or pricing data, and available cost or pricing information that permits realistic estimates of the probable costs of performance [@CL-EST-1153]. The last example is that performance uncertainties can be identified, their cost impact can be reasonably estimated, and the contractor is willing to accept a firm fixed price representing assumption of the risks involved [@CL-EST-1154]. The source text printed for this card sets no dollar threshold for choosing the type.

## Worked example

Synthetic example. The numbers are made up.

A contractor agrees to deliver 100 units at a firm fixed price of 1,000 each, a total price of 100,000. Its estimate of cost was 90,000. If actual cost comes in at 95,000, the contractor keeps the 5,000 gap between price and cost as profit, and if cost reaches 105,000 it absorbs the 5,000 loss. The price does not move either way, because it is not tied to the contractor's cost experience.

## Never confuse

A firm-fixed-price contract is not a fixed-price contract with economic price adjustment. The first has a price that is not adjusted for the contractor's cost experience [@CL-EST-1150]. The second provides for upward and downward revision of the stated price when specified contingencies occur [@CL-EST-1155]. The types of adjustment and when a contract may use one are on [Fixed-price contracts with economic price adjustment: types and use](est-fixed-price-epa-types-and-use.md), and the auditor's view is on [Economic price adjustments in proposals and proposal audits](est-economic-price-adjustments.md). A level-of-effort term contract is described separately in [Firm-fixed-price, level-of-effort term contracts](../../close-revrec/revenue/cls-firm-fixed-price-level-of-effort.md), and the wider contract-type map is in [Contract types](../../contract-finance/billing-payment/ctf-contract-types.md).
