## Purpose

Shows how the contracting officer chooses among price analysis, cost analysis and cost realism when evaluating a proposal.

## The rule

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Instrument | Codified FAR [@CL-EST-0110] |
| Codified citation | FAR 15.404-1 [@CL-EST-0111] |
| RFO citation | RFO FAR 15.404 [@CL-EST-3391]; RFO FAR 15.404-1 [@CL-EST-3397]; RFO FAR 15.404-2 [@CL-EST-3395]; RFO FAR 15.404-3 [@CL-EST-3399]; RFO FAR 15.404-4(a) [@CL-EST-2257] |
| Edition date | Not established. |
| Text status | Final [@CL-EST-0110] |
| Applies to | Proposals evaluated to reach a fair and reasonable price [@CL-EST-0110] |

The objective is a final agreed-to price that is fair and reasonable [@CL-EST-0110]. Price analysis is used when certified cost or pricing data are not required, while cost analysis tests individual cost elements when certified data are required [@CL-EST-0111; @CL-EST-0112].

## How it works

Cost analysis may also test data other than certified data for cost reasonableness or cost realism when price analysis alone cannot establish a fair and reasonable price [@CL-EST-0113]. The first two price analysis techniques are the preferred techniques [@CL-EST-9541]. However, if the contracting officer determines that information on competitive proposed prices or previous contract prices is not available or is insufficient to determine that the price is fair and reasonable, the contracting officer may use any of the remaining techniques as appropriate to the circumstances applicable to the acquisition [@CL-EST-9542; @CL-EST-0114]. Normally, adequate price competition establishes a fair and reasonable price [@CL-EST-0119]. Cost realism analysis is required on cost-reimbursement contracts and produces a probable cost that is used to determine best value [@CL-EST-0115; @CL-EST-0116]. Cost realism analyses may also be used on competitive fixed-price incentive contracts or, in exceptional cases, on other competitive fixed-price-type contracts when new requirements may not be fully understood by competing offerors, there are quality concerns, or past experience indicates that contractors' proposed costs have resulted in quality or service shortfalls [@CL-EST-9543; @CL-EST-9544]. Results of the analysis may be used in performance risk assessments and responsibility determinations [@CL-EST-9545]. However, proposals are still evaluated using the criteria in the solicitation, and the offered prices are not adjusted as a result of the analysis [@CL-EST-0117]. Recommendations or conclusions regarding the Government's review or analysis of an offeror's or contractor's proposal are not disclosed to the offeror or contractor without the concurrence of the contracting officer [@CL-EST-0118].

The FAR's general definitions say that cost realism means that the costs in an offeror's proposal are realistic for the work to be performed, reflect a clear understanding of the requirements, and are consistent with the various elements of the offeror's technical proposal [@CL-EST-9271]. The RFO model text of FAR 2.101 defines cost realism in the same words as the codified definition, apart from the printed form of the dash and the apostrophe [@CL-EST-9348; @CL-EST-9271].

Under the RFO text, DoD contracting officers use the RFO FAR part 15 text in place of the codified text under Class Deviation 2026-O0048, whose Revision 1 took effect immediately and supersedes the March 5, 2026 version [@CL-EST-3176] [@CL-EST-3177]. RFO FAR 15.404(a) keeps the objective of a final agreed-to price that is fair and reasonable [@CL-EST-0110; @CL-EST-3391]. Price analysis must still be used when certified cost or pricing data are not required [@CL-EST-0111; @CL-EST-3392], and when a fair and reasonable price cannot be determined through price analysis alone, cost analysis may also be used to evaluate data other than certified cost or pricing data to determine cost reasonableness or cost realism [@CL-EST-0113; @CL-EST-3393]. RFO FAR 15.404(a)(3) says cost analysis must be used when certified cost or pricing data are required and price analysis must be used to verify that the overall price offered is fair and reasonable [@CL-EST-3394], where the codified sentence says price analysis should be used to verify the overall price [@CL-EST-3414]. RFO FAR 15.404-2(a) says cost analysis includes the review and evaluation of any separate cost elements and profit or fee in an offeror's or contractor's proposal, as needed to determine a fair and reasonable price or to determine cost realism [@CL-EST-3395], and the application of judgment to determine how well the proposed costs represent what the cost of the contract should be, assuming reasonable economy and efficiency [@CL-EST-3417]. RFO FAR 15.404(a)(4) also says cost realism must be used when contemplating the award of a cost-reimbursement contract [@CL-EST-3396]. In RFO FAR 15.404-1(c) the techniques at 15.404-1(b)(1) and (2) are the preferred methods for price analysis [@CL-EST-9541; @CL-EST-3397], and if the contracting officer determines that information on competitive proposed prices or previous contract prices is not available or is insufficient to determine that the price is fair and reasonable, the contracting officer may use any of the remaining techniques as appropriate to the circumstances applicable to the acquisition [@CL-EST-0114; @CL-EST-3398; @CL-EST-3415]. RFO FAR 15.404-3(b) keeps the requirement that cost realism analyses be performed on cost-reimbursement contracts to determine the probable cost of performance for each offeror [@CL-EST-0115; @CL-EST-3399], with the probable cost used for evaluation to determine the best value [@CL-EST-0116; @CL-EST-3400]. Cost realism analyses may also be used on competitive fixed-price incentive contracts [@CL-EST-9543; @CL-EST-3401] or, in exceptional cases, on other competitive fixed-price-type contracts when new requirements may not be fully understood by competing offerors, there are quality concerns, or past experience indicates that contractors' proposed costs have resulted in quality or service shortfalls [@CL-EST-9544; @CL-EST-3416], and the results may be used in performance risk assessments and responsibility determinations, but proposals must be evaluated using the criteria in the RFP and the offered prices must not be adjusted as a result of the analysis [@CL-EST-9545; @CL-EST-0117; @CL-EST-3402]. Recommendations or conclusions regarding the Government's review or analysis of a proposal must still not be disclosed to the offeror or contractor without the concurrence of the contracting officer [@CL-EST-0118; @CL-EST-3403]. RFO FAR 15.404-4(a) says that, at a minimum, the technical analysis must examine the types and quantities of material proposed and the need for the types and quantities of labor hours and the labor mix [@CL-EST-2257]; the text names no official who performs that analysis.

## Worked example

Synthetic example. The numbers are made up.

A cost-reimbursement proposal offers 800,000, of which 100,000 is for labor hours the technical review found unnecessary. The realism analysis raises the base labor rate and trims those hours, producing a probable cost of 760,000. Evaluation uses the 760,000 probable cost, not the 800,000 offered figure.

## Never confuse

Price analysis applies when certified data are not required, while cost analysis evaluates individual cost elements when they are required [@CL-EST-0111; @CL-EST-0112]. Cost realism is a separate step for cost-reimbursement contracts, where probable cost rather than the offered price drives the evaluation [@CL-EST-0115; @CL-EST-0116].

The codified text says cost analysis is used to evaluate the reasonableness of individual cost elements when certified cost or pricing data are required [@CL-EST-0112]; RFO FAR 15.404(a)(3) states the requirement without those words [@CL-EST-3394], and RFO FAR 15.404-2(a) says cost analysis includes the review and evaluation of any separate cost elements [@CL-EST-3395].
