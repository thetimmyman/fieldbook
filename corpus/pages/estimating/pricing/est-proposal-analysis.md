## Purpose

Shows how the contracting officer chooses among price analysis, cost analysis and cost realism when evaluating a proposal.

## The rule

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Instrument | Codified FAR [@CL-EST-0110] |
| Codified citation | FAR 15.404-1 [@CL-EST-0111] |
| RFO citation | Not established. |
| Edition date | Not established. |
| Text status | Final [@CL-EST-0110] |
| Applies to | Proposals evaluated to reach a fair and reasonable price [@CL-EST-0110] |

The objective is a final agreed-to price that is fair and reasonable [@CL-EST-0110]. Price analysis is used when certified cost or pricing data are not required, while cost analysis tests individual cost elements when certified data are required [@CL-EST-0111; @CL-EST-0112].

## How it works

Cost analysis may also test data other than certified data for cost reasonableness or cost realism when price analysis alone cannot establish a fair and reasonable price [@CL-EST-0113]. The first two price analysis techniques are preferred, but when competitive or historical price information is unavailable or insufficient the officer may use any remaining technique [@CL-EST-0114]. Cost realism analysis is required on cost-reimbursement contracts and produces a probable cost that is used to determine best value [@CL-EST-0115; @CL-EST-0116]. Cost realism analysis may also be used on competitive fixed-price incentive contracts, and its results may be used in performance risk and responsibility assessments, but proposals are still evaluated on the solicitation criteria and offered prices are not adjusted as a result of the analysis [@CL-EST-0117]. Review conclusions are not shared with the offeror without the concurrence of the contracting officer [@CL-EST-0118].

## Worked example

Synthetic example. The numbers are made up.

A cost-reimbursement proposal offers 800,000, of which 100,000 is for labor hours the technical review found unnecessary. The realism analysis raises the base labor rate and trims those hours, producing a probable cost of 760,000. Evaluation uses the 760,000 probable cost, not the 800,000 offered figure.

## Never confuse

Price analysis applies when certified data are not required, while cost analysis evaluates individual cost elements when they are required [@CL-EST-0111; @CL-EST-0112]. Cost realism is a separate step for cost-reimbursement contracts, where probable cost rather than the offered price drives the evaluation [@CL-EST-0115; @CL-EST-0116].

## Company calibration

Which proposals expected in the next two quarters may need certified cost or pricing data, and so require cost analysis rather than price analysis. [@Q-047]
