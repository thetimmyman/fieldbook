## Purpose

Shows what a should-cost review examines, why it differs from a traditional evaluation, and what it produces for the negotiation file.

## The rule

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Instrument | Codified FAR [@CL-EST-0223] |
| Codified citation | FAR 15.407-4 [@CL-EST-0223] |
| RFO citation | RFO FAR 15.405-4 [@CL-EST-3451; @CL-EST-3454; @CL-EST-3456; @CL-EST-3458] and RFO FAR 15.105-6 [@CL-EST-3462] |
| Edition date | Not established. |
| Text status | Final [@CL-EST-0223] |
| Applies to | Cost analysis [@CL-EST-9548] of the contractor's existing work force, methods, materials, equipment, real property, operating systems, and management [@CL-EST-0224], run as a program review or an overhead review [@CL-EST-0226] |

A should-cost review is a specialized form of cost analysis [@CL-EST-9548] that differs from traditional evaluation methods because it does not assume that the contractor's historical costs reflect efficient and economical operation [@CL-EST-0223]. It evaluates the economy and efficiency of the contractor's existing work force, methods, materials, equipment, real property, operating systems, and management [@CL-EST-0224], using a multi-functional team of Government contracting, contract administration, pricing, audit, and engineering representatives [@CL-EST-0225].

## How it works

There are two types, a program review and an overhead review, and they may be performed together or independently [@CL-EST-0226]. A program review evaluates significant elements of direct costs, such as material and labor, and associated indirect costs, usually associated with the production of major systems [@CL-EST-9549], and when it is run against a contractor proposal a separate audit report on the proposal is required [@CL-EST-0227]. An overhead review evaluates indirect costs, such as fringe benefits, shipping and receiving, real property, and equipment, depreciation, plant maintenance and security, taxes, and general and administrative activities [@CL-EST-0228], is normally used to evaluate and negotiate a forward pricing rate agreement with the contractor, and requires a separate audit report [@CL-EST-0229]. When a program review is planned, the contracting officer should state that fact in the acquisition plan or acquisition plan updates and in the solicitation [@CL-EST-0232]. In acquisitions for which a program review is conducted, a separate program should-cost review team report, prepared in accordance with agency procedures, is required [@CL-EST-9550]. After completing the negotiation, the contracting officer must provide the administrative contracting officer a report of any identified uneconomical or inefficient practices together with a report of correction or disposition agreements reached with the contractor [@CL-EST-0230], and must establish a follow-up plan to monitor the correction [@CL-EST-0231].

Under the RFO text, DoD contracting officers use the RFO FAR part 15 text in place of the codified text under Class Deviation 2026-O0048, whose Revision 1 took effect immediately and supersedes the March 5, 2026 version [@CL-EST-3176] [@CL-EST-3177]. RFO FAR 15.405-4(a)(1) says should-cost reviews are a specialized form of cost analysis and differ from traditional evaluation methods because they do not assume that a contractor's historical costs reflect efficient and economical operation [@CL-EST-3451; @CL-EST-0223], that these reviews evaluate the economy and efficiency of the contractor's existing work force, methods, materials, equipment, real property, operating systems, and management [@CL-EST-3452; @CL-EST-0224], and that they are accomplished by a multi-functional team of Government contracting, contract administration, pricing, audit, and engineering representatives [@CL-EST-3453; @CL-EST-0225]. RFO FAR 15.405-4(a)(2) says there are two types of should-cost reviews, program should-cost review and overhead should-cost review, and that these should-cost reviews may be performed together or independently [@CL-EST-3454; @CL-EST-0226]. RFO FAR 15.405-4(b)(1) says a program should-cost review is used to evaluate significant elements of direct costs, such as material and labor, and associated indirect costs, usually associated with the production of major systems, and that when it is conducted relative to a contractor proposal a separate audit report on the proposal is required [@CL-EST-3455; @CL-EST-3456; @CL-EST-0227]. RFO FAR 15.405-4(c)(1) says an overhead should-cost review is used to evaluate indirect costs, such as fringe benefits, shipping and receiving, real property, and equipment, depreciation, plant maintenance and security, taxes, and general and administrative activities, that it is normally used to evaluate and negotiate an FPRA (forward pricing rate agreement) with the contractor, and that when it is conducted a separate audit report is required [@CL-EST-3457; @CL-EST-3458; @CL-EST-0228; @CL-EST-0229]. RFO FAR 15.405-4(b)(4) says that in acquisitions for which a program should-cost review is conducted a separate program should-cost review team report, prepared in accordance with agency procedures, is required [@CL-EST-3459], that after completing the negotiation contracting officers must provide the administrative contracting officer (ACO) a report of any identified uneconomical or inefficient practices, together with a report of correction or disposition agreements reached with the contractor [@CL-EST-3460; @CL-EST-0230], and that contracting officers must establish a follow-up plan to monitor the correction of the uneconomical or inefficient practices [@CL-EST-3461; @CL-EST-0231]. RFO FAR 15.105-6 says that when a program should-cost review is planned in accordance with 15.405-4, the contracting officer should state this fact in the acquisition plan and in the RFP, where codified FAR 15.407-4(b)(5) says in the acquisition plan or acquisition plan updates and in the solicitation [@CL-EST-3462; @CL-EST-0232].

## Worked example

Synthetic example. The numbers are made up.

A contractor proposes an overhead rate built on three indirect pools. A Government team reviews those pools, recommends four corrective actions, and the parties negotiate a forward pricing rate agreement from that analysis. The review produces a separate audit report, and the follow-up plan tracks whether each action is carried out. The answer is that this is an overhead should-cost review, not a proposal audit: it is normally used to evaluate and negotiate a forward pricing rate agreement and itself requires a separate audit report.

## Never confuse

A should-cost review does not assume that the contractor's historical costs reflect efficient and economical operation [@CL-EST-0223]; these reviews evaluate the economy and efficiency of the contractor's existing work force, methods, materials, equipment, real property, operating systems, and management [@CL-EST-0224]. When a program should-cost review is conducted relative to a contractor proposal, a separate audit report on the proposal is required [@CL-EST-0227]. The contracting officer shall establish a follow-up plan to monitor the correction of the uneconomical or inefficient practices [@CL-EST-0231].

The statement that a program review is planned is found in RFO FAR 15.105-6, which names the acquisition plan and the RFP [@CL-EST-3462], while codified FAR 15.407-4(b)(5) names the acquisition plan or acquisition plan updates and the solicitation [@CL-EST-0232].
