## Purpose

Shows what a should-cost review examines, why it differs from a traditional evaluation, and what it produces for the negotiation file.

## The rule

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Instrument | Codified FAR [@CL-EST-0223] |
| Codified citation | FAR 15.407-4 [@CL-EST-0223] |
| RFO citation | Not established. |
| Edition date | Not established. |
| Text status | Final [@CL-EST-0223] |
| Applies to | Cost analysis of a contractor's operation, run as a program review or an overhead review [@CL-EST-0226] |

A should-cost review is a specialized form of cost analysis that differs from traditional evaluation methods because it does not assume that the contractor's historical costs reflect efficient and economical operation [@CL-EST-0223]. It evaluates the economy and efficiency of the contractor's existing work force, methods, materials, equipment, real property, operating systems, and management [@CL-EST-0224], using a multi-functional team of Government contracting, contract administration, pricing, audit, and engineering representatives [@CL-EST-0225].

## How it works

There are two types, a program review and an overhead review, and they may be performed together or independently [@CL-EST-0226]. A program review evaluates significant direct costs such as material and labor together with associated indirect costs, and when it is run against a contractor proposal a separate audit report on the proposal is required [@CL-EST-0227]. An overhead review evaluates indirect costs such as fringe benefits, depreciation, and general and administrative activities [@CL-EST-0228], is normally used to evaluate and negotiate a forward pricing rate agreement with the contractor, and requires a separate audit report [@CL-EST-0229]. When a program review is planned, the contracting officer should state that fact in the acquisition plan or acquisition plan updates and in the solicitation [@CL-EST-0232]. In an acquisition with a program review, after negotiation the contracting officer provides the administrative contracting officer a report of identified uneconomical or inefficient practices together with a report of correction or disposition agreements reached with the contractor [@CL-EST-0230], and establishes a follow-up plan to monitor correction [@CL-EST-0231].

## Worked example

Synthetic example. The numbers are made up.

A contractor proposes an overhead rate built on three indirect pools. A Government team reviews those pools, recommends four corrective actions, and the parties negotiate a forward pricing rate agreement from that analysis. The review produces a separate audit report, and the follow-up plan tracks whether each action is carried out. The answer is that this is an overhead should-cost review, not a proposal audit: it is normally used to evaluate and negotiate a forward pricing rate agreement and itself requires a separate audit report.

## Never confuse

A should-cost review is not a pass through the contractor's historical costs to test their reasonableness; it asks whether the operation itself is economical and efficient [@CL-EST-0223]. It is also not the audit of the proposal. Where a program review runs against a contractor proposal, a separate audit report on the proposal is still required [@CL-EST-0227]. And a report of findings is not the end of the work: correction of uneconomical practices is monitored under a follow-up plan [@CL-EST-0231].
