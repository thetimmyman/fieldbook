## Purpose

Shows how a direct material estimate is built and checked: what counts as direct material, the consolidated bill of materials, the sources used to price it, and the use of price-reduction patterns for subcontract and material costs.

Direct materials include raw materials, purchased parts, subcontracted items, tooling, packaging, freight and other material directly identifiable to contract performance [@CL-EST-0924]. Under FAR 15.4, a consolidated bill of material is a comprehensive cost schedule that aggregates all discrete parts, materials and services required across the entire proposal into one priced document, officially called a consolidated priced summary of materials and services [@CL-EST-0687]. Auditors need to ensure the contractor includes a complete consolidated bill of material for proper evaluation [@CL-EST-0688].

Historical patterns of price drops constitute cost or pricing data under DFARS, and contractors must analyze, disclose and reflect them in their proposals through appropriate decrement factors [@CL-EST-0691].

## How it works

The basic estimating procedures include: estimating total quantity requirements, determine raw material requirements and unit conversions, price the items at current estimates, and adjust those prices for projected cost trends and purchase volumes [@CL-EST-0925]. Auditors should analyze individual material estimates from a qualitative perspective to ensure the proposed material effectively satisfies the Government's requirements [@CL-EST-0926]. Estimates are built from one or a combination of reliable baseline sources, including historical cost records and improvement curves [@CL-EST-0927]. A properly constructed bill of material is the foundational baseline, detailing raw materials, component parts and scrap or spoilage allowances [@CL-EST-0928]. Contractors may use both engineering and manufacturing bills of material, but the engineering one is preferred for audit verification because of its link to drawings and technical specifications [@CL-EST-0689], and manufacturing bills can add detail if potential cost duplication is carefully monitored [@CL-EST-0929]. Estimates for completely new products or prototypes need higher scrutiny for duplicated part requirements and typically need technical specialist help [@CL-EST-0930]. Prices can come from different sources such as standard costs, earlier purchase order prices adjusted for current quantities and current vendor quotations evaluated for competitive solicitation and reasonableness [@CL-EST-0690]. To confirm the priced bill of material, auditors verify prices against competing supplier quotes, recent historical costs and available inventory, while considering the type of subcontract or purchase order to be awarded and assessing consistency in estimating practices across proposals and compliance with applicable CAS [@CL-EST-0931; @CL-EST-1229]. Where scrap factors or common supply loading factors are applied, auditors must verify that the same costs are not duplicated in indirect cost pools, in noncompliance with CAS 401 or CAS 402 [@CL-EST-0693]. Auditors evaluate whether decrement factors are representative, accurate and consistently applied across the relevant quotation bases [@CL-EST-0932]. Auditors recommend corrective action on inefficient purchasing practices and, if the contractor fails to account for known historical price reduction trends, calculate and apply a decrement factor to adjust the proposed material and subcontract costs [@CL-EST-0692].

## Worked example

Synthetic example. The numbers are made up.

A proposal prices a part at a vendor quote of 100 per unit. The vendor's last several quotes to the same contractor fell steadily after the first lot. The contractor applies a decrement to the quote and discloses the pattern. Another line carries a scrap allowance in the bill of material, and the auditor checks that the same scrap cost is not also sitting in an overhead pool.

## Never confuse

A decrement factor reflects a historical pattern of price drops that the contractor must analyze, disclose and reflect in its proposal [@CL-EST-0691]. A bill of material is also not just a parts list: it is the baseline for the estimate [@CL-EST-0928], and the engineering version is the preferred one for audit verification [@CL-EST-0689]. Other direct costs are covered in [Other direct costs](est-other-direct-costs.md).
