## Purpose

Shows what an auditor looks for in the cost accounting system that feeds a price proposal, the three estimating approaches contractors mainly use, and who decides on technical specialist help.

The contractor's cost accounting system provides the primary baseline for price proposals, and the accounting data used to develop estimated costs must be valid and reliable [@CL-EST-0682]. In evaluating a proposal the auditor considers whether the system accurately segregates direct costs from indirect expenses, accumulates actual costs using methods consistent with estimating practices, and maintains internal controls that prevent systemic estimating errors [@CL-EST-0683]. The manual's example is a lot costing system with weak controls over job lot cutoffs, which can give inaccurate lot cost data and inequitable results when lot cost trends are used for follow-on procurement [@CL-EST-0919].

## How it works

Contractors primarily rely on three approaches to prepare estimates [@CL-EST-0684]. The detailed method builds verifiable costs from extensive bottom-up data such as drawings, bills of materials and production workloads [@CL-EST-0684]. The comparison method modifies actual costs from similar items by isolating differences and accounting for cost trends [@CL-EST-0685]. The roundtable method relies on the subjective judgment and consensus of departmental experts when historical data is absent; it is fast and inexpensive but produces less supportable estimates and frequently requires technical assistance to evaluate [@CL-EST-0686]. Where estimates are ratio-based, auditors must ensure that they adjust for complexity, production rates and performance periods while excluding past or present inefficient practices [@CL-EST-0920].

The proposal format and information to be submitted when certified cost or pricing data is required are prescribed by FAR Part 15 and DFARS Part 215 [@CL-EST-0921]. The auditor keeps sole professional responsibility for the audit opinion, including determining the need for any technical evaluation [@CL-EST-0922]. The auditor needs to determine that need during audit planning so there is enough time to include the evaluation in the report [@CL-EST-0923].

Under the RFO text, DoD contracting officers use the RFO FAR part 31 text in place of the codified text [@CL-CMP-9350], effective immediately under Revision 1 signed on 2026-08-04 [@CL-CMP-9351]. RFO FAR 31.003 says contractors needing assistance in developing or improving their accounting systems and procedures may request a copy of the Defense Contract Audit Agency Pamphlet number 7641.90, Information for Contractors [@CL-EST-9236].

## Worked example

Synthetic example. The numbers are made up.

A contractor estimates a new part three ways. A bottom-up build from drawings is the detailed method. A scaled copy of last year's similar part is the comparison method. A meeting of department leads that agrees a number with no history is the roundtable method. The first two can be traced to data, the third rests on judgment and frequently needs technical help to evaluate.

## Never confuse

The system that keeps actual costs and the method that builds an estimate are different things, but they must line up: actual costs need to be accumulated in ways consistent with estimating practices [@CL-EST-0683]. Parametric and ratio techniques are covered in [Parametric estimating](../wbs-analogy/est-parametric-estimating.md), and the audit of the whole estimating system in [Estimating systems](est-estimating-system-review.md).
