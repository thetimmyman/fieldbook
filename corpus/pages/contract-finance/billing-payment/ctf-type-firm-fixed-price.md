## Purpose

A firm-fixed-price contract establishes an agreed price that does not change with cost experience, placing all cost risk and potential profit or loss on the contractor. From a finance view, profit is simply the spread between the agreed price and the actual performance costs incurred.

## The rule

A firm-fixed-price contract provides for a price that is not subject to any adjustment on the basis of the contractor's cost experience, placing maximum risk and full responsibility for all costs and resulting profit or loss on the contractor [@CL-EST-1150].

The type is suitable for acquiring commercial products, commercial services, or other supplies or services bought on reasonably definite functional or detailed specifications, when fair and reasonable prices can be established at the outset [@CL-EST-1151; @CL-EST-1152]. Pricing can be established through adequate price competition, reasonable price comparisons with prior competitive purchases or purchases supported by cost or pricing data, or available cost information permitting realistic estimates of probable performance costs [@CL-EST-1152; @CL-EST-1153]. It is also suitable when performance uncertainties can be identified, reasonable estimates of their cost impact can be made, and the contractor is willing to accept a firm fixed price representing assumption of the risks involved [@CL-EST-1154].

## How it works

Profit is whatever remains between the agreed price and the costs the contractor incurs. Solicitations and fixed-price contracts under which the government provides progress payments based on costs include the clause at FAR 52.232-16, Progress Payments [@CL-CTF-4406].

In the level-of-effort term variant, the contractor provides a specified level of effort over a stated period of time on work stated only in general terms, and the government pays a fixed dollar amount [@CL-CLS-0562]. Payment under that variant is based on the effort expended rather than on the results achieved [@CL-CLS-0564]. It may be used only when the work required cannot otherwise be clearly defined, the required level of effort is identified and agreed upon in advance, and there is reasonable assurance that the intended result cannot be achieved by expending less than the stipulated effort [@CL-CLS-0565]. See [Firm-fixed-price contracts: risk and suitability](../../estimating/escalation/est-firm-fixed-price-contracts.md), [Firm-fixed-price, level-of-effort term contracts](../../close-revrec/revenue/cls-firm-fixed-price-level-of-effort.md), and [Selecting a contract type: the range of types and the policies](../../estimating/pricing/est-selecting-contract-types.md).

## Worked example

Synthetic example. The numbers are made up.

A contractor enters into a firm-fixed-price contract with an agreed price of 1,000,000 dollars. If actual performance costs finish at 900,000 dollars, the contractor keeps the difference between the 1,000,000 dollar price and the 900,000 dollar cost, realizing 100,000 dollars in profit. If actual performance costs instead rise to 1,050,000 dollars, the contract price remains 1,000,000 dollars, resulting in a loss of 50,000 dollars that the contractor absorbs in full because the price does not move. This example is illustrative only, and the terms of the contract itself govern.

## Never confuse

A firm-fixed-price contract does not adjust price based on cost experience, unlike a fixed-price incentive contract where profit and final price adjust by a formula based on final negotiated cost relative to target cost [@CL-EST-1150; @CL-EST-1568]. It must also not be confused with time-and-materials or labor-hour contracts, which are not fixed-price contracts [@CL-CTF-2359]. Nor is it equivalent to a firm-fixed-price level-of-effort term contract, where payment follows effort expended rather than results achieved [@CL-CLS-0564].
