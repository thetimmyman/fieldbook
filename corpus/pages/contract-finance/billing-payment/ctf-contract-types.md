## Purpose

Explains how the contract type changes billing, risk and the rules finance must watch.

## The rule

On a cost-reimbursement contract the government pays allowable costs as they are incurred, with indirect costs billed at provisional rates and settled after final rates are set [@S-0006-v1, § 52.216-7]. When such a contract is fully funded and carries the Limitation of Cost clause, the contractor must notify the contracting officer before costs pass a set share of the estimated cost [@CL-CTF-0003]. On a fixed-price contract the contractor bears the cost risk, and payment follows delivery, with financing such as progress payments where the contract allows it [@CL-CTF-0001].

## How it works

Finance reads the contract type first, because it decides how invoices are built, which costs are allowable, which notices are owed and how much risk sits with the company. Cost-type work needs incurred cost tracking, provisional billing rates and an annual final rate proposal; fixed-price work needs cost-to-complete control and financing requests. See [Limitation of Cost](../funding/ctf-limitation-of-cost.md) and [Contract financing at a glance](../financing/ctf-financing-overview.md).

## Worked example

Synthetic example. The numbers are made up.

A cost-type contract has an estimated cost of 2 million. By month ten the contractor expects to exceed the notice point within the next two months, so it notifies the contracting officer before spending further, rather than waiting until the estimate is used up.

## Never confuse

The Limitation of Cost clause applies to fully funded cost contracts; the Limitation of Funds clause applies to incrementally funded ones. A provisional billing rate is an interim rate for invoices, not the final rate.
