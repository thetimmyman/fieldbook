## Purpose

Shows what bonds the Contracting Officer may require under a ship repair Master Agreement and the Government's rights when the contractor defaults.

## The rule

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Instrument | Codified DFARS [@CL-CTF-1963; @CL-CTF-1964] |
| Codified citation | DFARS 252.217-7008 and DFARS 252.217-7009 [@CL-CTF-1963; @CL-CTF-1964] |
| RFO citation | Not established. |
| Edition date | Not established. |
| Text status | Final [@CL-CTF-1963] |
| Applies to | Job orders under a ship repair Master Agreement that includes the Bonds clause or the Default clause [@CL-CTF-1963; @CL-CTF-1964] |

Even if the solicitation does not require bonds, the Contracting Officer may nevertheless require a performance and payment bond, in form, amount and with a surety acceptable to the Contracting Officer [@CL-CTF-1963]. Where a performance and payment bond is required, the offer price is increased upon the award of the job order by an amount not to exceed the premium of a corporate surety bond [@CL-CTF-1985]. If the solicitation does not require a bid bond, the offeror may not include in the price any contingency to cover the premium of such a bond [@CL-CTF-1984]. If the solicitation requires a bid bond, the offeror may furnish instead an annual bid bond or an annual performance and payment bond, or evidence of either [@CL-CTF-2387].

One ground on which the Government may terminate the whole or any part of a job order by written notice of default is the contractor's failure to make delivery of the supplies or to perform the services within the time specified in a job order or any extension, subject to the excusable-cause paragraph [@CL-CTF-1964; @CL-CTF-2161; @CL-CTF-1966]. The clause also lists failure to make progress so as to endanger performance of the job order and failure to perform any of the other provisions of the agreement or a job order [@CL-CTF-2161]. Causes beyond the contractor's control and without its fault or negligence include acts of God or of the public enemy, acts of the Government, fires, floods, epidemics, quarantine restrictions, strikes, freight embargoes and unusually severe weather [@CL-CTF-2162].

## How it works

Except for defaults of subcontractors, the contractor is not liable for any excess costs if the failure to perform arises from causes beyond its control and without its fault or negligence [@CL-CTF-1966]. A subcontractor default has its own rule: if the default arises from causes beyond the control of both the contractor and the subcontractor, and without the fault or negligence of either, the contractor is not liable for excess costs [@CL-CTF-2151], unless the subcontractor's supplies or services were obtainable from other sources in time to perform the job order on schedule [@CL-CTF-2152]. For delay in completing a job order within the time specified or any extension, the contractor pays, in place of actual damage, the amount, if any, set in the job order for each calendar day of delay, prorated to the nearest hour for fractional days [@CL-CTF-2158; @CL-CTF-1965]. The contractor is not charged with liquidated damages when the delay arises out of causes beyond its control and without its fault or negligence [@CL-CTF-2155]. Subject to the Disputes clause, the Contracting Officer ascertains the facts and the extent of the delay and extends the time for performance when the findings of fact justify an extension [@CL-CTF-2156]. If the Government terminates the job order, the contractor is also liable, in addition to excess costs, for liquidated damages accruing until the Government may reasonably obtain completion of the work [@CL-CTF-2157].

After a default termination the Government may, on terms and in a manner the Contracting Officer deems appropriate, arrange for completion of the terminated work at plants the Contracting Officer designates, including the contractor's plant [@CL-CTF-1976]. The contractor continues the performance of the job order to the extent not terminated [@CL-CTF-2388]. If the work is to be completed at the plant, the Government may use all tools, machinery, facilities and equipment of the contractor that the Contracting Officer determines necessary for that purpose [@CL-CTF-2389]. The excess-cost comparison starts from the cost to the Government of the work procured or completed, adjusted to exclude the effect of changes made after termination, and sets it against the price fixed for the work under the job order [@CL-CTF-1977]. Where it exceeds that price, adjusted for changes made before termination, the contractor, or its surety, if any, is liable for the excess [@CL-CTF-1978]. The Government's rights and remedies under the clause are not exclusive and are in addition to any other rights and remedies provided by law under the agreement [@CL-CTF-2391].

The Government may also require the contractor to transfer title and delivery of completed supplies and of the partially completed supplies and materials the clause describes [@CL-CTF-1979]. These are limited to what the contractor has specifically produced or specifically acquired for the terminated part of the job order [@CL-CTF-1980]. Upon direction of the Contracting Officer, the contractor protects and preserves property in its possession in which the Government has an interest [@CL-CTF-2390]. The Government pays the job order price for completed items of work delivered to and accepted by it, and the amount agreed with the Contracting Officer for manufacturing materials delivered to and accepted by it [@CL-CTF-2159]. The agreed amount also covers protection and preservation of property, and failure to agree is a dispute concerning a question of fact under the Disputes clause [@CL-CTF-2160]. If it is later determined that the contractor was not in default, or that the default was excusable, the parties' rights and obligations are the same as for a termination for the convenience of the Government [@CL-CTF-1981]. Separately, if a surety fails to submit requested reports on its financial condition or otherwise becomes unacceptable to the Government, the Contracting Officer may require the contractor to furnish whatever additional security the Contracting Officer determines necessary to protect the interests of the Government and of persons supplying labor or materials in the performance of the work contemplated under the Master Agreement [@CL-CTF-1986; @CL-CTF-9588].

## Worked example

Synthetic example. The numbers are made up.

A job order sets liquidated damages at 2,000 for each calendar day of delay. The contractor finishes 4 days late. The delay is not excusable: it did not arise from causes beyond the contractor's control and without its fault or negligence, and the Contracting Officer's findings of fact do not justify an extension. With no termination, the damages are 8,000. Had the 4 days come from a cause such as unusually severe weather beyond the contractor's control and without its fault or negligence, no liquidated damages would be charged and the Contracting Officer would extend the time if the findings justified it. If the Government had terminated the job order, liquidated damages would also keep accruing until it could reasonably obtain completion, on top of the excess cost. In a separate case, the Government terminates a job order for failure to deliver on time and has the work completed at the contractor's plant, using the tools and machinery the Contracting Officer finds necessary, while the contractor protects and preserves, as directed, the Government property in its possession and continues any part of the job order not terminated. After the adjustments the clause requires, the completion cost is 1,150,000 and the job order price is 1,000,000. The contractor, or its surety, is liable for the 150,000 excess, unless the failure arose from causes beyond the contractor's control and without its fault or negligence. If the failure came from a subcontractor's default, the contractor escapes the excess only if that default was beyond the control and without the fault of both parties and the subcontractor's items could not have been obtained elsewhere in time. If it is later decided that the default was excusable, the rights and obligations are the same as for a termination for convenience.

## Never confuse

Liquidated damages for delay and excess costs after termination are different exposures. Liquidated damages are the daily amount, if any, set in the job order, and they are not charged for delay arising from causes beyond the contractor's control and without its fault or negligence [@CL-CTF-1965; @CL-CTF-2155]. After a termination they continue to accrue in addition to excess costs [@CL-CTF-2157]. Excess costs are shielded by the excusable-cause paragraph when the failure arises from causes beyond the contractor's control and without its fault or negligence, except for defaults of subcontractors [@CL-CTF-1966]. A bond is a third matter, and the Contracting Officer may require one even when the solicitation does not [@CL-CTF-1963]. Title that passes on a default termination, limited to items specifically produced or acquired for the terminated part, is different from title that passes to the Government under the payments clause when progress payments are made [@CL-CTF-1980; @CL-CTF-1960]; see [Ship repair master agreement: title, progress payments and discharge of liens (DFARS)](ctf-dfars-ship-repair-title-payments-and-liens.md). A default later found excusable is treated as if the termination had been for the convenience of the Government [@CL-CTF-1981]; see [Termination for convenience clauses and the contracts they go in](../../close-revrec/revenue/cls-termination-for-convenience-clauses.md) and [Termination for default: effect, notices and repurchase against the contractor](../../close-revrec/revenue/cls-termination-for-default-effect-and-repurchase.md). See also [Default clauses for fixed-price contracts and when each goes in](../../close-revrec/revenue/cls-fixed-price-default-clauses.md) and [Surety-takeover agreements after a default termination](../../close-revrec/revenue/cls-surety-takeover-agreements.md).
