## Purpose

Shows when the Government may terminate a fixed-price supply or service contract for default, how the cure notice works, who pays the excess reprocurement costs, what the Government pays for and takes after a default, when a failure is excused, and when a default is converted to a convenience termination.

Subject to paragraphs (c) and (d) of the clause, the Government may by written notice of default terminate the contract in whole or in part if the contractor fails to deliver the supplies or to perform the services within the time specified in the contract or any extension [@CL-CTF-2082]. The two further grounds are failing to make progress so as to endanger performance of the contract and failing to perform any of the other provisions of the contract, and each is followed by a cross-reference to subparagraph (a)(2) of the clause [@CL-CTF-2083]. The Government's right to terminate on those two grounds may be exercised if the contractor does not cure the failure within 10 days, or more if the contracting officer authorizes it in writing, after receiving the notice [@CL-CTF-2084]. The cure period runs after receipt of the notice from the contracting officer specifying the failure [@CL-CTF-2242].

If the Government terminates, it may acquire, under the terms and in the manner the contracting officer considers appropriate, supplies or services similar to those terminated, and the contractor will be liable to the Government for any excess costs for those supplies or services [@CL-CTF-2085]. However, the contractor shall continue the work not terminated [@CL-CTF-2239]. Except for defaults of subcontractors at any tier, the contractor is not liable for any excess costs if the failure to perform arises from causes beyond the control and without the fault or negligence of the contractor [@CL-CTF-2086]. Examples of such causes include acts of God or of the public enemy, acts of the Government in either its sovereign or contractual capacity, fires, floods, epidemics, quarantine restrictions, strikes, freight embargoes and unusually severe weather [@CL-CTF-2240]. In each instance the failure to perform must be beyond the control and without the fault or negligence of the contractor [@CL-CTF-2241].

## How it works

The Government must first give written notice of default, and the grounds for progress and other provisions work through a cure period [@CL-CTF-2082; @CL-CTF-2084]. After termination the Government may reprocure, and the extra cost falls on the contractor unless the excusable-delay rule applies [@CL-CTF-2085; @CL-CTF-2086].

In the paragraph on a subcontractor's default, the conditions are that the failure to perform is caused by the default of a subcontractor at any tier, the cause of the default is beyond the control of both the contractor and the subcontractor, and the cause is without the fault or negligence of either [@CL-CTF-2105]. Then the contractor is not liable for any excess costs for failure to perform, unless the subcontracted supplies or services were obtainable from other sources in sufficient time for the contractor to meet the required delivery schedule [@CL-CTF-2087].

If the contract is terminated for default, the Government may require the contractor to transfer title and deliver to the Government, as directed by the contracting officer, completed supplies [@CL-CTF-2103]. The list also covers partially completed supplies and materials, parts, tools, dies, jigs, fixtures, plans, drawings, information, and contract rights, which the clause collectively calls manufacturing materials, that the contractor has specifically produced or acquired for the terminated portion of the contract [@CL-CTF-2104]. Upon direction of the contracting officer, the contractor must also protect and preserve property in its possession in which the Government has an interest [@CL-CTF-2243]. Title rules for property are also on [Title to Government-furnished and contractor-acquired property](../../close-revrec/fixed-assets/cls-title-to-government-furnished-and-contractor-acquired-property.md).

On payment, the Government pays the contract price for completed supplies delivered and accepted, and the contractor and the contracting officer agree on the amount of payment for manufacturing materials delivered and accepted and for the protection and preservation of the property [@CL-CTF-2101]. Failure to agree will be a dispute under the Disputes clause, and the Government may withhold from these amounts any sum the contracting officer determines to be necessary to protect the Government against loss because of outstanding liens or claims of former lien holders [@CL-CTF-2102]. The Disputes clause is on [Disputes clause](../../close-revrec/revenue/cls-disputes-clause.md).

If it is later determined that the contractor was not in default, or that the default was excusable, the rights and obligations of the parties are the same as if the termination had been issued for the convenience of the Government [@CL-CTF-2088].

## Worked example

Synthetic example. The numbers are made up.

A contractor owes 100 units at 1,000 each. It delivers 40 units that the Government accepts, and then stops. After a default termination the Government pays the contract price for the 40 delivered and accepted units, which is 40,000, and buys 60 similar units at 1,300 each. The extra paid is 300 a unit, so the excess costs for the 60 units are 18,000. If the contractor has completed 10 more units that it has not delivered, the Government may require title to them and their delivery. If there are liens on that property, the Government may withhold the sum the contracting officer determines is needed to protect it from the lien claims.

If a flood that was beyond the contractor's control and without its fault stopped the factory, the contractor owes no excess costs. If the failure was caused by a subcontractor that could have been replaced in time, the excuse does not reach the excess costs. If a later determination finds the contractor was not in default, the parties stand as if the Government had terminated for convenience.

## Never confuse

A termination for default is not a termination for convenience, but a later finding that the contractor was not in default, or that the default was excusable, converts the rights and obligations to the convenience position [@CL-CTF-2088]. The cure period applies to the progress and other-provision grounds [@CL-CTF-2084], and the on-time delivery ground is not named there. Payment on default covers delivered and accepted supplies at the contract price, while manufacturing materials are paid at an agreed amount [@CL-CTF-2101]. For the settlement that follows a convenience termination, see [Termination for Convenience of the Government (Fixed-Price): settlement](ctf-termination-for-convenience-settlement.md), and for the effect and repurchase rules see [Termination for default: effect, notices and repurchase against the contractor](../../close-revrec/revenue/cls-termination-for-default-effect-and-repurchase.md). The research and development version is on [Default under fixed-price research and development contracts](ctf-default-fixed-price-research-and-development.md), and the architect-engineer clause is on [Termination of fixed-price architect-engineer and personal services contracts](ctf-termination-architect-engineer-and-personal-services.md). Payment terms for a transportation contract with Alternate I are not stated on this card [@CL-CLS-1060].
