## Purpose

Shows the three types of indefinite-delivery contract, how an indefinite-quantity contract sets its minimum and maximum, and when the multiple-award preference applies.

## The rule

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Instrument | Codified FAR [@CL-CTF-1207] |
| Codified citation | FAR 16.501-2 and FAR 16.504 [@CL-CTF-1207; @CL-CTF-1209] |
| RFO citation | Not established. |
| Edition date | Not established. |
| Text status | Final [@CL-CTF-1207] |
| Applies to | Indefinite-delivery contracts, and indefinite-quantity contracts for the minimum, maximum and multiple-award rules [@CL-CTF-1207; @CL-CTF-1209; @CL-CTF-1211] |

There are three types of indefinite-delivery contracts: definite-quantity contracts, requirements contracts, and indefinite-quantity contracts [@CL-CTF-1207]. The appropriate type may be used to acquire supplies or services when the exact times and/or exact quantities of future deliveries are not known at the time of contract award [@CL-CTF-1208].

An indefinite-quantity contract must require the Government to order and the contractor to furnish at least a stated minimum quantity of supplies or services, and the contractor must furnish any additional quantities, if ordered, not to exceed the stated maximum [@CL-CTF-1209]. To ensure that the contract is binding, the minimum quantity must be more than a nominal quantity, but it should not exceed the amount that the Government is fairly certain to order [@CL-CTF-1210]. Except for indefinite-quantity contracts for advisory and assistance services as provided in FAR 16.504(c)(2), the contracting officer must, to the maximum extent practicable, give preference to making multiple awards of indefinite-quantity contracts under a single solicitation for the same or similar supplies or services to two or more sources [@CL-CTF-1211; @CL-CTF-1484]. Except as provided in FAR 16.504(c)(2)(ii), if an indefinite-quantity contract for advisory and assistance services exceeds 3 years and $20 million, including all options, the contracting officer must make multiple awards unless: (A) the contracting officer or other official designated by the head of the agency determines in writing, as part of acquisition planning, that multiple awards are not practicable, and must determine that only one contractor can reasonably perform the work because either the scope of work is unique or highly specialized or the tasks so integrally related [@CL-CTF-1252; @CL-CTF-0804; @CL-CTF-1146]; (B) the contracting officer or other official designated by the head of the agency determines in writing, after the evaluation of offers, that only one offeror is capable of providing the services required at the level of quality required [@CL-CTF-1146; @CL-CTF-1147]; or (C) only one offer is received [@CL-CTF-1147]. The requirements of paragraph (c)(2)(i) do not apply if the contracting officer or other official designated by the head of the agency determines that the advisory and assistance services are incidental and not a significant component of the contract [@CL-CTF-1253].

## How it works

An indefinite-quantity contract requires the Government to order and the contractor to furnish at least a stated minimum quantity, and the contractor to furnish any additional quantities, if ordered, not to exceed the stated maximum [@CL-CTF-1209]. The contracting officer must not use the multiple award approach if: (1) only one contractor is capable of providing performance at the level of quality required because the supplies or services are unique or highly specialized [@CL-CTF-1212]; (2) based on the contracting officer's knowledge of the market, more favorable terms and conditions, including pricing, will be provided if a single award is made [@CL-CTF-1148]; (3) the expected cost of administration of multiple contracts outweighs the expected benefits of making multiple awards [@CL-CTF-1148]; (4) the projected orders are so integrally related that only a single contractor can reasonably perform the work [@CL-CTF-1149]; (5) the total estimated value of the contract is at or below the simplified acquisition threshold [@CL-CTF-1213; @CL-CTF-1149]; or (6) multiple awards would not be in the best interests of the Government [@CL-CTF-1149]. No task or delivery order contract in an amount estimated to exceed $150 million (including all options) may be awarded to a single source unless the head of the agency determines in writing that: (i) the task or delivery orders expected under the contract are so integrally related that only a single source can reasonably perform the work [@CL-CTF-1214; @CL-CTF-1150]; (ii) the contract provides only for firm-fixed-price task or delivery orders for products for which unit prices are established in the contract or services for which prices are established in the contract for the specific tasks to be performed [@CL-CTF-1150; @CL-CTF-1151]; (iii) only one source is qualified and capable of performing the work at a reasonable price to the Government [@CL-CTF-1151]; or (iv) it is necessary in the public interest to award the contract to a single source due to exceptional circumstances, in which case the head of the agency must notify Congress within 30 days after any determination under paragraph (c)(1)(ii)(D)(1)(iv) [@CL-CTF-1278]. The requirement for a determination for a single-award contract greater than $150 million is in addition to any applicable requirements of Subpart 6.3 and is not applicable for architect-engineer services awarded pursuant to Subpart 36.6 [@CL-CTF-1254].

## Worked example

Synthetic example. The numbers are made up.

An indefinite-quantity contract for spare parts states a minimum of 100 units and a maximum of 1,000 units. The Government places a first order for 40 units and a second order for 150 units. The answer: the contract bound the Government to order at least 100 units, which the two orders already exceed, and the contractor must furnish further quantities, if ordered, only up to 1,000 units. Finance checks the contract and each order for the funds actually obligated.

## Never confuse

An indefinite-quantity contract differs from a requirements contract in what the Government must order rather than in form: an indefinite-quantity contract must require the Government to order and the contractor to furnish at least a stated minimum quantity [@CL-CTF-1209], whereas a requirements contract instead provides for filling all actual purchase requirements of designated Government activities for supplies or services during a specified contract period from one contractor [@CL-CTF-1215]; see [Requirements contracts](ctf-requirements-contracts.md). Funding limits are on [Contract funding: incremental funding policy](ctf-incremental-funding-policy.md) and [Limitation of Funds clause](ctf-limitation-of-funds.md). The clauses that go into a solicitation for each type are on [Indefinite-delivery solicitation provisions and clauses](ctf-indefinite-delivery-solicitation-clauses.md), and how the contract type is chosen is on [Negotiating and selecting contract type](ctf-contract-type-negotiation-and-selection.md). The effect of contract type on billing and risk is on [Contract types and what they mean for finance](../billing-payment/ctf-contract-types.md).
