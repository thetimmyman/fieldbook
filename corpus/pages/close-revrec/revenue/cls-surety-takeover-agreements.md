## Purpose

Shows when the Government may enter a written agreement with a surety that completes a defaulted contract, and what such an agreement must cover. The default clauses that lead to such a termination are on [Default clauses for fixed-price contracts and when each goes in](cls-fixed-price-default-clauses.md).

The takeover procedures apply primarily, but not solely, to fixed-price construction contracts terminated for default [@CL-CLS-1007]. Because there may be conflicting demands for the defaulting contractor's assets, including unpaid prior earnings (retained percentages and unpaid progress estimates) [@CL-CLS-1604], a surety may include a takeover agreement in its proposal, fixing the surety's rights to payment from those funds, and the contracting officer may, but not before the effective date of termination, enter into a written agreement with the surety [@CL-CLS-1008].

## How it works

Any takeover agreement must require the surety to complete the contract and the Government to pay the surety's costs and expenses up to the balance of the contract price unpaid at the time of default, subject to the conditions the section lists, all of which apply [@CL-CLS-1009]. One listed condition is that any unpaid earnings of the defaulting contractor, including retained percentages and progress estimates for work accomplished before termination, must be subject to debts due the Government by the contractor, except as the sentence goes on to provide [@CL-CLS-1050]. The exception lets unpaid earnings be used to pay the completing surety its actual costs and expenses incurred in completing the work, but not including its payments and obligations under the payment bond given in connection with the contract [@CL-CLS-1010; @CL-CLS-0187]. Another binds the surety to the contract's liquidated damages terms for delays in completion of the work, unless the delays are excusable under the contract [@CL-CLS-1011]. A third is that if the contract proceeds have been assigned to a financing institution, the surety must not be paid from unpaid earnings, unless the assignee provides written consent [@CL-CLS-1012]. A fourth is that the contracting officer must not pay the surety more than the amount it expended completing the work and discharging its liabilities under the defaulting contractor's payment bond [@CL-CLS-1013], and payments to the surety to reimburse it for discharging its liabilities under the payment bond of the defaulting contractor must be only on authority of mutual agreement among the Government, the defaulting contractor, and the surety, a determination of the Comptroller General as to payee and amount, or an order of a court of competent jurisdiction [@CL-CLS-0188; @CL-CLS-0189].

## Worked example

Synthetic example. The numbers are made up.

A defaulted construction contract has 400,000 of the price still unpaid. A surety offers to finish the work, and a takeover agreement is signed after the termination takes effect. No financing institution holds an assignment of the contract proceeds. The surety spends 350,000 completing the work, and the contractor owes the Government 30,000 on other debts. The Government pays the surety its 350,000 of actual completion costs, which is within the unpaid balance. The remaining 50,000 of unpaid earnings stays subject to the 30,000 of Government debts.

## Never confuse

A takeover agreement is not a repurchase of the undelivered work from another source at the defaulted contractor's expense. The repurchase and excess-cost rules are on [Termination for default: effect, notices and repurchase against the contractor](cls-termination-for-default-effect-and-repurchase.md). The contracting officer must not pay the surety more than the amount it expended completing the work and discharging its liabilities under the defaulting contractor's payment bond [@CL-CLS-1013].
