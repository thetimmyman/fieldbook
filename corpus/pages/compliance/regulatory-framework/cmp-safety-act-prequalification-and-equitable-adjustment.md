## Purpose

Explains what a pre-qualification designation notice does for an offeror, how its alternates treat contingent offers and filing, and when a contractor can obtain an equitable adjustment after a SAFETY Act denial [@CL-CMP-5592; @CL-CMP-5595].

## The rule

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Instrument | Codified FAR [@CL-CMP-5592; @CL-CMP-5595] |
| Codified citation | FAR 52.250-4 and FAR 52.250-5 [@CL-CMP-5592; @CL-CMP-5595] |
| RFO citation | RFO FAR 52.250-4 [@CL-CMP-4487; @CL-CMP-4488; @CL-CMP-4489; @CL-CMP-4490; @CL-CMP-4616; @CL-CMP-4617; @CL-CMP-4618]; RFO FAR 52.250-5 [@CL-CMP-4619; @CL-CMP-4620; @CL-CMP-4621; @CL-CMP-4622; @CL-CMP-4623; @CL-CMP-4624; @CL-CMP-4625] |

A pre-qualification designation notice states that the technology to be procured affirmatively or presumptively satisfies the technical criteria for a qualified anti-terrorism technology, and it authorizes streamlined applications with expedited processing [@CL-CMP-5593; @CL-CMP-5594].

The provision defines a SAFETY Act designation as a determination by DHS that a particular anti-terrorism technology constitutes a qualified anti-terrorism technology under the SAFETY Act [@CL-CMP-6238]. A qualified anti-terrorism technology is any technology designed, developed, modified, procured or sold for the purpose of preventing, detecting, identifying or deterring acts of terrorism or limiting the harm such acts might otherwise cause, for which a SAFETY Act designation has been issued [@CL-CMP-6234]. For that definition, technology means any product, equipment, service (including support services), device or technology (including information technology) or any combination of the foregoing [@CL-CMP-6235]. Design services, consulting services, engineering services, software development services, software integration services, threat assessments, vulnerability studies and other analyses relevant to homeland security may be deemed a technology [@CL-CMP-6236]. A SAFETY Act certification is a determination by DHS that a qualified anti-terrorism technology for which a designation has been issued is an approved product for homeland security [@CL-CMP-6237], and an approved product for homeland security is one that will perform as intended, conforms to the seller's specifications, and is safe for use as intended [@CL-CMP-6254]. A block certification is a SAFETY Act certification of a technology class that DHS has determined to be an approved class of approved products for homeland security, and a block designation is a SAFETY Act designation of a technology class that DHS has determined to be a qualified anti-terrorism technology [@CL-CMP-6255; @CL-CMP-6256]. The SAFETY Act creates certain liability limitations for claims arising out of, relating to, or resulting from an act of terrorism where qualified anti-terrorism technologies have been deployed, and it also confers other important benefits [@CL-CMP-6257]. SAFETY Act designation and certification are designed to support effective technologies aimed at preventing, detecting, identifying or deterring acts of terrorism, or limiting the harm that such acts might otherwise cause, and which also meet other prescribed criteria [@CL-CMP-6258].

An act of terrorism is an act determined to have met the listed requirements, or such other requirements as the Secretary of Homeland Security defines and specifies, which include that the act is unlawful and causes harm, including financial harm, to a person, property or entity in the United States [@CL-CMP-6231]. For a domestic United States air carrier or a United States-flag vessel, or a vessel based principally in the United States on which United States income tax is paid and whose insurance coverage is subject to regulation in the United States, the harm may be in or outside the United States [@CL-CMP-6232]. An act of terrorism also uses or attempts to use instrumentalities, weapons or other methods designed or intended to cause mass destruction, injury or other loss to citizens or institutions of the United States [@CL-CMP-6233]. The equitable adjustment clause itself defines a SAFETY Act designation as a determination by DHS that a particular anti-terrorism technology constitutes a qualified anti-terrorism technology under the SAFETY Act [@CL-CMP-6242]. It likewise defines a SAFETY Act certification as a determination by DHS that a qualified anti-terrorism technology for which a designation has been issued is an approved product for homeland security [@CL-CMP-6243], and a qualified anti-terrorism technology as any technology designed, developed, modified, procured or sold for the purpose of preventing, detecting, identifying or deterring acts of terrorism or limiting the harm such acts might otherwise cause, for which a SAFETY Act designation has been issued [@CL-CMP-6244]. For that definition, technology means any product, equipment, service (including support services), device or technology (including information technology) or any combination of the foregoing [@CL-CMP-6249], and design services, consulting services, engineering services, software development services, software integration services, threat assessments, vulnerability studies and other analyses relevant to homeland security may be deemed a technology [@CL-CMP-6250]. An approved product for homeland security is one that will perform as intended, conforms to the seller's specifications, and is safe for use as intended [@CL-CMP-6251]. A block certification is a SAFETY Act certification of a technology class that DHS has determined to be an approved class of approved products for homeland security, and a block designation is a SAFETY Act designation of a technology class that DHS has determined to be a qualified anti-terrorism technology [@CL-CMP-6252; @CL-CMP-6253]. In that clause, an act of terrorism is an act determined to have met the listed requirements, or such other requirements as the Secretary of Homeland Security defines and specifies, which include that the act is unlawful and causes harm, including financial harm, to a person, property or entity in the United States [@CL-CMP-6246]. For a domestic United States air carrier or a United States-flag vessel, or a vessel based principally in the United States on which United States income tax is paid and whose insurance coverage is subject to regulation in the United States, the harm may be in or outside the United States [@CL-CMP-6247]. An act of terrorism also uses or attempts to use instrumentalities, weapons or other methods designed or intended to cause mass destruction, injury or other loss to citizens or institutions of the United States [@CL-CMP-6248].

Where DHS has issued a notice for the technology to be acquired, the notice is attached to the solicitation and contains essential information [@CL-CMP-5722]. It contains a detailed description of and specification for the technology it covers, and a statement that the technology satisfies the technical criteria to be deemed a qualified anti-terrorism technology [@CL-CMP-5723]. It also states that the offeror's proposed technology either may presumptively or will qualify for the issuance of a designation, provided the offeror complies with the terms and conditions in the notice and its application is approved [@CL-CMP-5724]. It states the period within which DHS will act on an application submitted under it, and lists the portions of the application that selected awardees must complete and submit and the time periods for those submissions [@CL-CMP-5725]. It states its expiration date and any other terms and conditions, and offerors should read it carefully to make sure they comply with its terms if they plan on taking advantage of SAFETY Act coverage [@CL-CMP-5726]. All determinations by DHS are based on factors set forth in the SAFETY Act and its implementing regulations [@CL-CMP-6239]. A determination by DHS to issue, or not to issue, a SAFETY Act designation for a particular technology as a qualified anti-terrorism technology is not a determination that the technology meets, or fails to meet, the requirements of any solicitation issued by any Federal, State, local or tribal governments [@CL-CMP-6240]. Determinations by DHS on whether to issue a designation for technologies submitted for DHS review are based on the factors identified in 6 CFR 25.4(b) [@CL-CMP-6241].

In the pre-qualification provision, neither SAFETY Act designation nor certification is a requirement of the action, and whether to seek the benefits of the SAFETY Act for a proposed product or service is entirely up to the offeror [@CL-CMP-5727]. In the basic pre-qualification provision, a proposal in which pricing or any other terms or conditions are offered contingent on designation or certification of the proposed product or service will not be considered for award [@CL-CMP-5728].

## How it works

The first alternate substitutes a different paragraph (f). It authorizes offerors to submit proposals made contingent on SAFETY Act designation before award [@CL-CMP-5729]. An offeror whose offer is contingent also may submit an alternate offer without the contingency [@CL-CMP-5730]. The Government may not award a contract based on a contingent offer unless the offeror demonstrates before award that DHS has issued a designation for its technology [@CL-CMP-5731]. It reserves the right to award on a noncontingent offer before DHS resolves the offeror's application [@CL-CMP-5732].

The second alternate authorizes offerors to submit proposals presuming designation before or after award [@CL-CMP-5733]. An offeror is eligible for award only if, among other conditions, it files its designation application, limited to the scope of the applicable pre-qualification designation notice, within 15 days after submitting the proposal [@CL-CMP-5734]. The other conditions are pursuing the application in good faith and agreeing to obtain the amount of insurance DHS requires for issuing the designation [@CL-CMP-5735]. If DHS has not issued a designation to the successful offeror before contract award, the contracting officer will include the equitable adjustment clause, 52.250-5, in the resulting contract [@CL-CMP-5736].

In that clause, prices for the items covered by the pre-qualification designation notice, block designation or block certification were established presuming that DHS will issue a designation or certification for those items [@CL-CMP-5596]. To qualify for an equitable adjustment, the contractor must in good faith pursue obtaining SAFETY Act designation, or certification [@CL-CMP-5737]. It must likewise pursue the amount of insurance DHS requires for issuing any designation or certification [@CL-CMP-5738]. If DHS denies the application, the contractor may request an equitable adjustment within 30 days of DHS's notification of denial [@CL-CMP-5597].

The contracting officer must then either make an equitable adjustment to the contract price based on evidence of the resulting increase or decrease in the contractor's costs, and/or an equitable adjustment to other terms and conditions based on lack of designation or certification [@CL-CMP-5739]. The other option, at the sole option of the Government, is to terminate the contract for the convenience of the Government in place of an equitable adjustment [@CL-CMP-5740]. A failure to agree is a dispute under the Disputes clause, and unless first terminated the contractor continues performance during establishment of any equitable adjustment [@CL-CMP-5741].

Under the RFO text, DoD contracting officers use the RFO FAR part 52 text in place of the codified text under Class Deviation 2026-O0038, effective February 1, 2026 [@CL-CMP-9760] [@CL-CMP-9762]. The RFO text of the pre-qualification provision keeps the definition of a pre-qualification designation notice, which states that the technology to be procured affirmatively or presumptively satisfies the technical criteria and authorizes streamlined applications with expedited processing [@CL-CMP-4487; @CL-CMP-4488; @CL-CMP-5593; @CL-CMP-5594]. It keeps the rule that neither designation nor certification is a requirement of the action [@CL-CMP-4489; @CL-CMP-5727], and the basic provision's rule that a proposal with pricing or other terms contingent on designation or certification will not be considered for award [@CL-CMP-4490; @CL-CMP-5728]. Its first alternate keeps the rule that the Government may not award a contract based on an offer contingent upon receipt of designation before award unless the offeror demonstrates before award that DHS has issued a designation for its technology [@CL-CMP-4616; @CL-CMP-5731]. Its second alternate keeps the condition that an offeror is eligible for award only if it files its designation application, limited to the scope of the applicable pre-qualification designation notice, within 15 days after submitting the proposal [@CL-CMP-4617; @CL-CMP-5734], together with the conditions of good faith and obtaining the insurance DHS requires [@CL-CMP-4618; @CL-CMP-5735]. The RFO equitable adjustment clause keeps the statement that prices were established presuming DHS will issue a designation [@CL-CMP-4619; @CL-CMP-5596], the duty to pursue designation and the insurance DHS requires in good faith [@CL-CMP-4620; @CL-CMP-4621; @CL-CMP-5737; @CL-CMP-5738], and the 30-day window for requesting an equitable adjustment after DHS denies the application [@CL-CMP-4622; @CL-CMP-5597]. It says the contracting officer "will" either make an equitable adjustment or, at the sole option of the Government, terminate for convenience instead, where the codified clause says "shall" [@CL-CMP-4623; @CL-CMP-4624; @CL-CMP-4625; @CL-CMP-5739; @CL-CMP-5740].

## Worked example

Synthetic example. The numbers are made up.

Prices for a monitoring system were set at 1,200,000 on the assumption that a designation would issue, and the contract includes the equitable adjustment clause. The contractor pursued the designation and the required insurance in good faith. DHS denies the application, and the contractor requests an equitable adjustment 20 days after the notice of denial. The request is timely, because it came inside the 30-day window. The contracting officer must then either adjust the price on evidence of the change in the contractor's costs, or, at the Government's sole option, terminate for convenience instead. If the parties cannot agree, the matter is a dispute, and the contractor keeps performing unless the contract is terminated first.

Under the second alternate to the provision, a different offeror files its designation application 10 days after submitting its proposal, within the scope of the notice. That is inside the 15-day limit, and it also pursues the application in good faith and agrees to obtain the required insurance.

## Never confuse

A pre-qualification designation notice is not a designation. The notice is the Government's statement that the technology affirmatively or presumptively satisfies the technical criteria, and the offeror must still apply [@CL-CMP-5593; @CL-CMP-5594; @CL-CMP-5724]. The notice changes how an application is processed, while the equitable adjustment clause deals with the contract price and other terms when DHS denies the designation on which prices were based [@CL-CMP-5594; @CL-CMP-5596; @CL-CMP-5597; @CL-CMP-5739]. The equitable adjustment clause also gives no automatic payment: the contractor must have pursued designation and insurance in good faith, must request within the time limit, and the Government may, at its sole option, terminate for convenience instead [@CL-CMP-5737; @CL-CMP-5597; @CL-CMP-5740].

The coverage and block designation provisions are covered in [SAFETY Act coverage and block designation provisions](cmp-safety-act-coverage-and-block-designation.md).
