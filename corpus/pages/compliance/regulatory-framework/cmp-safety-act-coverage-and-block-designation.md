## Purpose

Explains the two SAFETY Act solicitation provisions, what an offeror may submit under them, the timing that applies and how a contingent offer affects award [@CL-CMP-5584; @CL-CMP-5587].

## The rule

The coverage-not-applicable provision records the Government's determination that, for purposes of the solicitation, the product or service being acquired is neither presumptively nor actually entitled to a pre-determination that it is a qualified anti-terrorism technology [@CL-CMP-5585]. That determination does not prevent sellers of technologies from applying for SAFETY Act protections in other contexts [@CL-CMP-5709]. A proposal that makes either acceptance or pricing contingent on SAFETY Act designation or certification of the proposed product or service will not be considered for award [@CL-CMP-5586].

The block provision applies where DHS has issued a block designation or block certification for the technology to be acquired under the solicitation; that document is attached to the solicitation and contains essential information [@CL-CMP-5713]. A block certification is a SAFETY Act certification of a technology class that DHS has determined to be an approved class of approved products for homeland security, and a block designation is a SAFETY Act designation of a technology class that DHS has determined to be a qualified anti-terrorism technology [@CL-CMP-5710; @CL-CMP-5711]. A SAFETY Act designation is a determination by DHS that a particular anti-terrorism technology constitutes a qualified anti-terrorism technology under the SAFETY Act [@CL-CMP-5712]. A SAFETY Act certification is a determination by DHS that a qualified anti-terrorism technology for which a designation has been issued is an approved product for homeland security [@CL-CMP-6222]. An approved product for homeland security is one that will perform as intended, conforms to the seller's specifications, and is safe for use as intended [@CL-CMP-6223].

A qualified anti-terrorism technology is any technology designed, developed, modified, procured or sold for the purpose of preventing, detecting, identifying or deterring acts of terrorism or limiting the harm such acts might otherwise cause, for which a SAFETY Act designation has been issued [@CL-CMP-6219]. For defining a qualified anti-terrorism technology, technology means any product, equipment, service (including support services), device or technology (including information technology) or any combination of the foregoing [@CL-CMP-6220]. Design services, consulting services, engineering services, software development services, software integration services, threat assessments, vulnerability studies and other analyses relevant to homeland security may be deemed a technology [@CL-CMP-6221].

An act of terrorism is an act determined to have met the listed requirements, or such other requirements as the Secretary of Homeland Security defines and specifies, which include that the act is unlawful and causes harm, including financial harm, to a person, property or entity in the United States [@CL-CMP-6216]. For a domestic United States air carrier or a United States-flag vessel, or a vessel based principally in the United States on which United States income tax is paid and whose insurance coverage is subject to regulation in the United States, the harm may be in or outside the United States [@CL-CMP-6217]. An act of terrorism also uses or attempts to use instrumentalities, weapons or other methods designed or intended to cause mass destruction, injury or other loss to citizens or institutions of the United States [@CL-CMP-6218].

The SAFETY Act creates certain liability limitations for claims arising out of, relating to, or resulting from an act of terrorism where qualified anti-terrorism technologies have been deployed, and it also confers other important benefits [@CL-CMP-6224]. SAFETY Act designation and certification are designed to support effective technologies aimed at preventing, detecting, identifying or deterring acts of terrorism, or limiting the harm that such acts might otherwise cause, and which also meet other prescribed criteria [@CL-CMP-6225]. For some classes of technologies, DHS may issue a block designation or certification to lessen the burdens of filing for designation or certification by not requiring applicants to provide certain information otherwise required [@CL-CMP-6226]. DHS may also issue a block designation or certification to offer expedited review of any application submitted under it, and block designations and certifications will be issued only for technologies that rely on established performance standards or defined technical characteristics [@CL-CMP-6227].

The attached document contains a detailed description of and specification for the technology it covers, and a listing of the portions of the SAFETY Act application kit that applicants must complete and submit [@CL-CMP-5714]. It states its expiration date and any other terms and conditions, and offerors should read it carefully to make sure they comply with its terms if they plan to take advantage of SAFETY Act coverage [@CL-CMP-5715].

All determinations by DHS are based on factors set forth in the SAFETY Act and its implementing regulations [@CL-CMP-6228]. A determination by DHS to issue, or not to issue, a SAFETY Act designation for a particular technology as a qualified anti-terrorism technology is not a determination that the technology meets, or fails to meet, the requirements of any solicitation issued by any Federal, State, local or tribal governments [@CL-CMP-6229]. Determinations by DHS on whether to issue a designation for technologies submitted for DHS review are based on the factors identified in 6 CFR 25.4(b) [@CL-CMP-6230].

## How it works

In the block provision, neither designation nor certification is a requirement of the action, and whether to seek the benefits of the SAFETY Act for a proposed product or service is entirely up to the offeror [@CL-CMP-5588]. In the basic block provision, a proposal in which pricing or any other terms or conditions are offered contingent on designation or certification of the proposed product or service will not be considered for award [@CL-CMP-5716].

The first alternate substitutes a different paragraph (f). It authorizes offerors to submit proposals made contingent on designation, or on certification where a block certification exists, before award [@CL-CMP-5589]. An offeror whose offer is contingent also may submit an alternate offer without the contingency [@CL-CMP-5717]. The Government may not award a contract based on a contingent offer unless the offeror demonstrates before award that DHS has issued a designation, or a certification where a block certification exists, for the offeror's technology [@CL-CMP-5590]. The Government reserves the right to award on a noncontingent offer before DHS resolves the offeror's application [@CL-CMP-5718].

The second alternate substitutes another paragraph (f). It authorizes offerors to submit offers presuming that designation, or certification where a block certification exists, will be obtained before or after award [@CL-CMP-5719]. An offeror is eligible for award only if it files its application, limited to the scope of the block designation or block certification, within 15 days after submitting the proposal [@CL-CMP-5591]. The conditions of eligibility also include pursuing the application in good faith and agreeing to obtain the amount of insurance DHS requires for issuing the designation or certification [@CL-CMP-5720]. If DHS has not issued a designation or certification to the successful offeror before contract award, the contracting officer will include the clause at 52.250-5 in the resulting contract [@CL-CMP-5721].

Under the RFO text, DoD contracting officers use the RFO FAR part 52 text in place of the codified text from February 1, 2026 [@CL-CMP-9760] [@CL-CMP-9762]. RFO FAR 52.250-2 keeps the Government's determination that, for the solicitation, the product(s) or service(s) being acquired are neither presumptively nor actually entitled to a pre-determination that they are qualified anti-terrorism technologies, as that term is defined by the Support Anti-terrorism by Fostering Effective Technologies Act of 2002 (SAFETY Act), sections 441 to 444 of title 6 of the United States Code [@CL-CMP-2535; @CL-CMP-9778; @CL-CMP-5585], the statement that this determination does not prevent sellers of technologies from applying for SAFETY Act protections in other contexts [@CL-CMP-2536; @CL-CMP-5709], and the rule that a proposal in which either acceptance or pricing is made contingent upon SAFETY Act designation or certification of the proposed product or service will not be considered for award [@CL-CMP-2537; @CL-CMP-5586]. RFO FAR 52.250-3(c) keeps the statement that DHS has issued a block designation or block certification for the technology to be acquired under the solicitation, and that it is attached to the solicitation and contains essential information [@CL-CMP-2538; @CL-CMP-5713], including a detailed description of and specification for the technology it covers and a listing of the portions of the SAFETY Act application kit that applicants must complete and submit [@CL-CMP-2543; @CL-CMP-5714], and its expiration date and any other terms and conditions, with offerors to read it carefully to make sure they comply with its terms if they plan to take advantage of SAFETY Act coverage [@CL-CMP-2544; @CL-CMP-5715]. RFO paragraph (a) keeps the definition of block certification as a SAFETY Act certification of a technology class that DHS has determined to be an approved class of approved products for homeland security [@CL-CMP-2539; @CL-CMP-5710], of block designation as a SAFETY Act designation of a technology class that DHS has determined to be a qualified anti-terrorism technology [@CL-CMP-2540; @CL-CMP-5711], of SAFETY Act designation as a determination by DHS, pursuant to sections 441(b) and 443(a) of title 6 of the United States Code and as further delineated in 6 CFR 25.4, that a particular anti-terrorism technology constitutes a qualified anti-terrorism technology under the SAFETY Act [@CL-CMP-2541; @CL-CMP-5712], and of SAFETY Act certification as a determination by DHS, pursuant to section 442(d) of title 6 of the United States Code and as further delineated in 6 CFR 25.9, that a qualified anti-terrorism technology for which a designation has been issued is an approved product for homeland security [@CL-CMP-2542; @CL-CMP-6222], where an approved product for homeland security will perform as intended, conform to the seller's specifications, and be safe for use as intended [@CL-CMP-9779; @CL-CMP-6223]. RFO paragraph (d) keeps the statement that a determination by DHS to issue, or not to issue, a SAFETY Act designation for a particular technology as a qualified anti-terrorism technology is not a determination that the technology meets, or fails to meet, the requirements of any solicitation issued by any Federal, State, local or tribal governments [@CL-CMP-2545; @CL-CMP-6229]. RFO paragraph (e) keeps the statement that neither SAFETY Act designation nor certification is a requirement of the action, and that whether to seek the benefits of the SAFETY Act for a proposed product or service is entirely up to the offeror [@CL-CMP-2546; @CL-CMP-5588]. RFO paragraph (f) of the basic provision keeps the rule that a proposal in which pricing or any other terms or conditions are offered contingent on SAFETY Act designation or certification of the proposed product or service will not be considered for award [@CL-CMP-2547; @CL-CMP-5716]. RFO Alternate I keeps the substitute paragraph (f) that authorizes offerors to submit proposals made contingent on SAFETY Act designation, or on certification where a block certification exists, before award [@CL-CMP-2548; @CL-CMP-5589], the offeror's option to submit an alternate offer without the contingency [@CL-CMP-2549; @CL-CMP-5717], the rule that, for an offer submitted contingent upon receipt of designation, or of certification where a block certification exists, prior to contract award [@CL-CMP-9780], the Government may not award a contract on that offer unless the offeror demonstrates before award that DHS has issued a designation, or a certification where a block certification exists, for the offeror's technology [@CL-CMP-2574; @CL-CMP-5590], and the Government's right to award on a noncontingent offer before DHS resolves the application [@CL-CMP-2575; @CL-CMP-5718]. RFO Alternate II keeps the substitute paragraph (f) that authorizes offerors to submit offers presuming that designation, or certification where a block certification exists, will be obtained before or after award [@CL-CMP-2580; @CL-CMP-5719]. It keeps the rule that an offeror is eligible for award only if it files its application, limited to the scope of the block designation or block certification, within 15 days after submitting the proposal [@CL-CMP-3756; @CL-CMP-5591], and the other eligibility conditions of pursuing the application in good faith and agreeing to obtain the amount of insurance DHS requires [@CL-CMP-3757; @CL-CMP-5720]. It keeps the rule that, if DHS has not issued a designation or certification to the successful offeror before contract award, the contracting officer will include the clause at 52.250-5 in the resulting contract [@CL-CMP-3758; @CL-CMP-5721].

## Worked example

Synthetic example. The numbers are made up.

An offeror prices a screening service at 4,000,000 and states that the price holds only if DHS designates the technology before award. The solicitation uses the block designation provision with no alternate, so a proposal that makes pricing contingent on designation is not considered, and the offeror must bid without the condition to stay in the competition.

A second solicitation uses the block provision with the second alternate. The offeror files its application, within the scope of the block designation, 12 days after it submits the proposal, pursues it in good faith and agrees to obtain the insurance DHS requires. It meets the 15-day limit and the other conditions, so it is eligible. An application filed 18 days after submission would not be eligible. If this offeror is the successful offeror and DHS has not designated its technology before the contract is awarded, the contracting officer includes the equitable adjustment clause.

## Never confuse

An optional benefit and an award condition are easily mixed up. In the block provision, seeking SAFETY Act benefits is up to the offeror, and in the coverage-not-applicable provision and the basic block provision a proposal made contingent on designation is not considered for award [@CL-CMP-5586; @CL-CMP-5716]. With the first alternate, a contingent proposal may be submitted, but the Government may not award on it unless the offeror shows before award that the designation was issued [@CL-CMP-5589; @CL-CMP-5590]. With the second alternate, the offer presumes designation, and eligibility turns on a timely, good-faith application and agreement to obtain the required insurance [@CL-CMP-5719; @CL-CMP-5591; @CL-CMP-5720].

The pre-qualification designation notice and the equitable adjustment clause are covered in [SAFETY Act pre-qualification notice and equitable adjustment](cmp-safety-act-prequalification-and-equitable-adjustment.md).
