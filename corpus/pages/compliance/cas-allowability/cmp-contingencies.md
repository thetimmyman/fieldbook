## Purpose

Shows when a contingency can be charged to a Government contract, and when it has to stay outside the cost estimate and be disclosed instead.

## The rule

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Instrument | Codified FAR [@CL-CMP-0751] |
| Codified citation | FAR 31.205-7 [@CL-CMP-0751] |
| RFO citation | Not established. |
| Edition date | Not established. |
| Text status | Final [@CL-CMP-0751] |
| Applies to | Contingencies, as the term is used in the cost principles subpart [@CL-CMP-0751] |

A contingency is a possible future event or condition, from known or unknown causes, whose outcome is indeterminable at the present time [@CL-CMP-0751]. For historical costing, contingency costs are generally unallowable, because that costing deals with costs already incurred and recorded on the contractor's books [@CL-CMP-0752].

Recognition for a past period survives in limited cases, such as terminations, where a contingency factor covers minor unsettled factors and expedites settlement [@CL-CMP-0753]. In estimates of future costs the rule splits in two. The first category is contingencies that may arise from presently known and existing conditions, the effects of which are foreseeable within reasonable limits of accuracy [@CL-CMP-1110]; they are included in the estimates so as to provide the best estimate of performance cost [@CL-CMP-0754]. The second category is contingencies that may arise from presently known or unknown conditions, the effect of which cannot be measured so precisely as to provide equitable results [@CL-CMP-1111]; they are excluded from the estimates under the several items of cost but should be disclosed separately, including the basis on which they are computed [@CL-CMP-0755].

## How it works

The contractor first decides whether the cost under examination is historical or an estimate of future cost, because the two are treated differently. Historical costing starts from the books, so a contingency has no recorded cost behind it and is generally unallowable, apart from the narrow past-period recognition for minor unsettled factors [@CL-CMP-0753]. For a forward estimate the contractor sorts each contingency into one of the two categories the principle describes [@CL-CMP-1110; @CL-CMP-1111]. Items in the first category are included in the estimate [@CL-CMP-0754], and items in the second category are excluded from the individual items of cost and should be disclosed separately with their basis, to facilitate the negotiation of appropriate contractual coverage [@CL-CMP-0755].

## Worked example

Synthetic example. The numbers are made up.

A proposal carries a 250,000 estimate for a production line. Anticipated rework of 15,000 is foreseeable, so it is built into the line. A pending dispute is estimated at 100,000, but its effect cannot be measured precisely, so that amount is left out of the line and disclosed separately with its basis.

## Never confuse

A contingency is not an incurred cost: it is a possible future event or condition whose outcome is indeterminable at the present time [@CL-CMP-0751]. Treatment turns on timing and category: in a forward estimate the first category of contingency is included and the second is excluded and disclosed separately [@CL-CMP-0754; @CL-CMP-0755], while contingency costs are generally unallowable in historical costing [@CL-CMP-0752]. Contingencies attached to a dispute are one common case; see [Costs of legal proceedings](cmp-legal-proceedings-costs.md).

## Company calibration

Whether the company keeps foreseeable contingencies inside its estimates and discloses the unmeasurable ones, with their basis, before negotiation [@Q-043].
