## Purpose

Restates the most consequential worked illustrations in the pension cost standard, with their facts and outcomes, so a reader sees how the limits bite.

In one illustration, a multiemployer defined-benefit plan under which the contractor pays six cents into the fund for each hour worked by the covered employees under a collective bargaining agreement shall be considered to be a defined-contribution pension plan [@CL-CMP-3396]. In another, the assignable cost allocable to cost objectives of the period is $29,000, the sum of $24,000 of benefits actually paid in that period and a $5,000 second annual installment amortizing the prior year's lump sum settlements [@CL-CMP-3397].

In the illustration of Contractor K, where the assignable cost limitation is $1.3 million, the contractor's assignable pension cost for 2017 is limited to $1.3 million [@CL-CMP-3398]. The standard applies that limit when the pension cost equals or exceeds the assignable cost limitation [@CL-CMP-3391]. In a second illustration of Contractor K, the pension cost computed for 2017 is $1.5 million, the assignable cost limitation is $1.7 million and the accumulated value of prepayment credits is $0 [@CL-CMP-3431]. Because that contractor cannot fund more than $1 million without an excise tax, which the standard does not permit to be a component of pension cost, the assignable pension cost for the period is limited to $1 million [@CL-CMP-3399; @CL-CMP-3430]. In a variant with $700,000 of accumulated prepayment credits, the amount of pension cost assigned to the current period shall not exceed $1,700,000, the sum of the $1 million maximum tax-deductible amount and the $700,000 accumulated value of prepayment credits [@CL-CMP-3432].

In the illustration of Contractor M, the contractor computes $1 million of pension cost for a cost accounting period but, pursuant to a waiver granted under the provisions of ERISA, is required to fund only $800,000 [@CL-CMP-3448]. The remaining $200,000 shall be accounted for as an assignable cost deficit and assigned to the next five cost accounting periods in accordance with the terms of the waiver [@CL-CMP-3400]. A shortfall without a waiver is treated differently: where only $800,000 was funded against $1 million of assigned pension cost for the period, only $800,000 may be allocated to the contractor's intermediate and final cost objectives [@CL-CMP-3401], and the remaining $200,000 of assigned cost, which has not been funded, shall be separately identified and maintained so that it will not be reassigned to any future accounting periods [@CL-CMP-3447].

In the illustration of Contractor P, the provisions limit the assigned cost allocable within the cost accounting period to the percentage of cost funded, $59,800 divided by $65,000 or 92 percent, which results in allocable cost of $92,000, being 92 percent of $100,000 [@CL-CMP-3402]. The facts there are those of the first Contractor P illustration except that the contribution to the Trust is $59,800 [@CL-CMP-3433]. In another illustration, at least 32 percent of benefit payments shall be made from sources other than the funding agency, so the contractor draws $238,000 from the funding agency assets and pays the remaining $112,000 using general corporate funds [@CL-CMP-3403].

The source also holds illustrations for the pension harmonization rule. In them, because the contractor separately computes pension costs by segment, or aggregation of segments, the applicability of the harmonization threshold is determined separately for Segment 1 and Segments 2 through 7 [@CL-CMP-3439]. The rest of those illustrations are not claimed here.

## How it works

The illustrations show several different brakes on pension cost. A limit set by the assignable cost limitation is shown in one case [@CL-CMP-3398]. A limit set by the amount that can be funded without an excise tax is shown in another [@CL-CMP-3399; @CL-CMP-3430]. Prepayment credits can raise that second limit, because the assigned cost may reach the sum of the maximum tax-deductible amount and the prepayment credits [@CL-CMP-3432; @CL-CMP-3427]. A funding shortfall that cuts what may be allocated is shown in a further case [@CL-CMP-3401]. A waiver changes the treatment of the same kind of shortfall, because the unfunded part is then an assignable cost deficit assigned to later periods [@CL-CMP-3400].

## Worked example

Synthetic example. The numbers are made up.

A qualified plan has an assignable cost limitation of 40, a computed pension cost of 50 and no prepayment credits, so the assigned cost is capped at 40. The maximum tax-deductible amount is 35, so the assigned cost is further capped at 35 and the 5 above that is assigned to future periods as an assignable cost deficit.

Separately, a contractor with a qualified plan funds 90 of 120 assigned cost with no waiver. Only 90 may be allocated to cost objectives, and the remaining 30 is separately identified and not reassigned to any future period.

## Never confuse

A cap from the assignable cost limitation is not the same as a cap from the tax-deductible funding level. In the second Contractor K illustration the limitation is $1.7 million but the assignable pension cost is capped at $1 million [@CL-CMP-3431; @CL-CMP-3399], and with $700,000 of prepayment credits the cap rises to $1,700,000 [@CL-CMP-3432]. A waiver is also not the same as a shortfall without one: with a waiver the unfunded part is carried as an assignable cost deficit [@CL-CMP-3400], and without one only the funded amount may be allocated [@CL-CMP-3401].
