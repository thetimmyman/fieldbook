## Purpose

Walks through the printed illustrations of home office pools and allocation bases, and says what the interpretation decides about allocating income taxes from a home office to segments. The rule itself is on [Allocation of home office expenses to segments](../cas-allowability/cmp-cas-403-home-office-allocation.md).

The first table in the illustrations lists some typical home office pools together with illustrative allocation bases that could be used in appropriate circumstances [@CL-CMP-2791]. Among the listed rows, data processing services has machine time and number of reports as illustrative bases, and centralized purchasing and subcontracting has number of purchase orders, value of purchases and number of items [@CL-CMP-2880]. Engineering policies has total engineering costs, engineering direct labor and number of drawings, and material and purchasing policies has number of purchase orders and value of purchases [@CL-CMP-2881]. The other rows are in the printed section. The listed allocation bases are illustrative, and other bases for allocating home office expenses to segments may be used if they are substantially in accordance with the beneficial or causal relationships the standard outlines [@CL-CMP-2792].

The interpretation's first criterion is that the taxes of any particular jurisdiction are allocated only to those segments that do business in the taxing jurisdiction [@CL-CMP-2793]. Its second criterion is that where there is more than one segment in a taxing jurisdiction, the taxes are allocated among those segments on the basis of the same factors used to determine taxable income for that jurisdiction [@CL-CMP-2794]. Segment book income is used as a factor in allocating income tax expense from a home office to segments only where the taxing jurisdiction expressly uses that amount in computing the income tax [@CL-CMP-2795].

## How it works

The illustrations offer example bases and do not fix them [@CL-CMP-2791; @CL-CMP-2792]. The interpretation then settles one question about income taxes, which is whether segment book income is a factor. Taxes go only to segments that do business in the taxing jurisdiction [@CL-CMP-2793]. Among several segments in one jurisdiction, they follow the factors that jurisdiction uses to determine taxable income [@CL-CMP-2794]. Book income counts only where the jurisdiction expressly uses it [@CL-CMP-2795].

## Worked example

Synthetic example. The numbers are made up.

A home office pays income tax to a State that taxes a fraction of total organization income by a statutory formula. Segment A and Segment B both do business there, and Segment C does not. The tax goes to A and B only, split by the factors the State uses. Book income is left out because the State does not use it.

## Never confuse

An illustrative base is not a required base, because other bases may be used if they are substantially in accordance with the beneficial or causal relationships the standard outlines [@CL-CMP-2792]. Segment book income is not a general factor for tax allocation, because it is used only where the taxing jurisdiction expressly uses it [@CL-CMP-2795]. The rule itself is on [Allocation of home office expenses to segments](../cas-allowability/cmp-cas-403-home-office-allocation.md). For the segment in the home office expenses illustration, the illustration says home office residual expenses should be in the general and administrative expense pool [@CL-CMP-2815]; see [Illustrations of the general and administrative pool and cost input base](../cas-allowability/cmp-cas-410-illustrations.md). In the five-segment research illustration, the costs are combined with other independent research and development costs that benefit the company as a whole and are allocated to all five segments by means of the same base by which the company allocates its residual home office expense costs of all segments [@CL-CMP-2899]; see [Illustrations of direct and indirect classification and of research and proposal costs](../cas-allowability/cmp-cas-418-and-420-illustrations.md).
