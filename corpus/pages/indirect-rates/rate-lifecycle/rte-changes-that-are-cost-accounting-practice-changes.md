## Purpose

Walks through each illustration in the Cost Accounting Standards Board rules of a change that meets the definition of a change to a cost accounting practice.

| RFO citation | Not established. |

The section lists illustrations of changes which meet the definition of a change to a cost accounting practice [@CL-RTE-0815]. Each illustration below is listed under that heading [@CL-RTE-0815]. Pension costs: a contractor that computed pension costs using the aggregate cost method before the change computes them using the unit credit method after it [@CL-RTE-0816]. Direct labor: direct labor cost was measured with only one component set at standard, and after the change both the time and rate components are set at standard [@CL-RTE-0817]. Small assets: the contractor changes its established criteria for capitalizing certain classes of tangible capital assets whose acquisition costs totaled $1 million per cost accounting period [@CL-RTE-0858]. Before the change, items with acquisition costs of between $200 and $400 per unit were capitalized and depreciated over a number of cost accounting periods [@CL-RTE-0818], and after the change the contractor charges the value of such assets to an indirect expense pool allocated to the cost objectives of the cost accounting period in which the cost was incurred [@CL-RTE-0819]. Depreciation: depreciation costs were assigned to cost accounting periods using an accelerated method, and after the change they are assigned using the straight line method [@CL-RTE-0820]. Asset lives: for classes of assets acquired prior to the effective date of CAS 409, the contractor identified the cost accounting periods for tangible capital assets using guideline class lives provided in an IRS revenue procedure [@CL-RTE-0821], and after the change it uses the expected actual lives based on past usage [@CL-RTE-0822].

G&A base: before the change, the contractor operating under Cost Accounting Standard 410 had been allocating its general and administrative expense pool to final cost objectives on a total cost input base in compliance with the Standard [@CL-RTE-0859]. Its business changes substantially such that there are significant new projects which have only insignificant quantities of material [@CL-RTE-0860]. After the addition of the new work, an evaluation of the changed circumstances reveals that continued use of a total cost input base would result in a significant distortion in the allocation of the G&A expense pool in relation to the benefits received [@CL-RTE-0861]. To remain in compliance with Standard 410, the contractor alters its G&A allocation base from a total cost input base to a value added base [@CL-RTE-0823]. Hardware: the contractor allocated the cost of purchased or requisitioned hardware directly to projects, and after the change it charges the cost to an indirect expense pool allocated to projects using an appropriate allocation base [@CL-RTE-0824]. Merged segments: the manufacturing overhead pool was allocated to final cost objectives using a direct labor hours base in segment A and a direct labor dollars base in segment B [@CL-RTE-0825], and after the merger of operations the combined segment decides to allocate the pool to all final cost objectives using a direct labor dollars base [@CL-RTE-0826]. For the final cost objectives of segment A, the pool will then be allocated using a direct labor dollars base instead of a direct labor hours base [@CL-RTE-0862].

## How it works

Each illustration gives the accounting treatment before the change and after the change [@CL-RTE-0816; @CL-RTE-0817; @CL-RTE-0820]. For the definition itself, see the [definitions card](../../compliance/cas-allowability/cmp-cost-accounting-practice-definitions.md).

## Worked example

Synthetic example. The numbers are made up.

A contractor depreciates a class of equipment costing 800,000 using an accelerated method. It moves the class to the straight line method. Total cost over the asset life is the same, but cost is assigned to different periods. This is a change to a cost accounting practice, like the depreciation illustration, because the method of assigning cost to cost accounting periods changed.
