## Purpose

Explains how cost-sharing arrangements and negotiated ceilings limit what the government pays for indirect costs.

## The rule

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Instrument | Codified FAR [@CL-RTE-0308] |
| Codified citation | FAR 42.707 [@CL-RTE-0308] |
| RFO citation | RFO FAR 42.506 [@CL-RTE-0926; @CL-RTE-0927] |
| Edition date | Not established. |
| Text status | Final [@CL-RTE-0308] |
| Applies to | Contracts where cost sharing is authorized or a negotiated rate ceiling is included [@CL-RTE-0309] |

Where cost sharing is authorized, the arrangement may call for the contractor to participate in the costs of the contract by accepting rates lower than the anticipated actual rates, and a negotiated rate ceiling may be included in the contract for prospective application [@CL-RTE-0308; @CL-RTE-0309]. Other situations may make a ceiling prudent too, for example a new or recently reorganized company with no past or recent record of incurred indirect costs [@CL-RTE-0310].

In the FAR's general definitions, cost sharing means an explicit arrangement under which the contractor bears some of the burden of reasonable, allocable, and allowable contract cost [@CL-RTE-9065].

When ceiling provisions are used, the contract must also provide that the Government is not obligated to pay any additional amount should the final indirect cost rates exceed the negotiated ceiling rates, and that the negotiated rates are reduced to conform with the lower rates if the final rates come in lower [@CL-RTE-0312; @CL-RTE-0313].

## How it works

In such situations an equitable ceiling covering the final indirect cost rates may be negotiated and specified in the contract [@CL-RTE-0311]. Once ceiling provisions are used, the contract carries both adjustments, so the Government pays nothing above the ceiling and the negotiated rates drop to a lower final rate [@CL-RTE-0312; @CL-RTE-0313].

Under the RFO text, DoD contracting officers use the RFO FAR part 42 text in place of the codified text because Class Deviation 2026-O0050 directs it, effective March 16, 2026 [@CL-CMP-9759] [@CL-CMP-9761]. RFO FAR 42.506(a) keeps the rule that cost-sharing arrangements, when authorized, may call for the contractor to participate in the costs of the contract by accepting indirect cost rates lower than the anticipated actual rates [@CL-RTE-0926; @CL-RTE-0308], and that in such cases a negotiated indirect cost rate ceiling may be incorporated into the contract for prospective application [@CL-RTE-0927; @CL-RTE-0309]. RFO FAR 42.506(b) says other situations may warrant the use of final indirect cost rate ceilings, such as when the contractor is a new or recently reorganized company with no indirect cost history, where the codified text says other situations "may make it prudent" and speaks of no past or recent record of incurred indirect costs [@CL-RTE-0928; @CL-RTE-0310]. RFO paragraph (b) ends with a contractor that seeks a competitive advantage by basing its proposal on unrealistically low indirect cost rates, likely to result in a cost overrun, and RFO paragraph (c) follows it [@CL-RTE-0929]; the printed paragraph (b) does not contain a sentence like the codified one that an equitable ceiling covering the final indirect cost rates may be negotiated and specified in the contract [@CL-RTE-0311]. RFO FAR 42.506(c)(1) says that, when ceiling provisions are utilized, the contract must also provide that the Government is not obligated to pay any additional amount should the final rates exceed the negotiated ceiling rates, where the codified text says "shall" [@CL-RTE-0930; @CL-RTE-0312]. RFO FAR 42.506(c)(2) says that, if final rates are less than the ceiling rates, the rates will be reduced to conform with the lower rates, without the word "negotiated" that the codified text uses [@CL-RTE-0931; @CL-RTE-0313]. RFO FAR 42.501 keeps the definition of a billing rate as an indirect cost rate established temporarily for interim reimbursement of incurred indirect costs and adjusted as necessary until final indirect cost rates are established [@CL-RTE-0932; @CL-RTE-0300].

## Worked example

Synthetic example. The numbers are made up.

A contractor accepts an overhead ceiling of 30 percent on a base of 2,000,000 while expecting an actual rate of 32 percent. The final rate is 33 percent, so the government pays 600,000 and nothing more, and the 60,000 above the ceiling stays with the contractor. Had the final rate been 28 percent, the negotiated rate would drop and the billable amount would fall to 560,000.

## Never confuse

A rate ceiling is not a billing rate. A billing rate is an indirect cost rate established temporarily for interim reimbursement and adjusted as necessary pending final rates [@CL-RTE-0300], while a ceiling limits what the Government will pay if the final rates exceed it [@CL-RTE-0312]. A ceiling is also not limited to cost sharing, because other situations may make one prudent [@CL-RTE-0310].

The codified text says that in the other situations an equitable ceiling covering the final indirect cost rates may be negotiated and specified in the contract [@CL-RTE-0311], while RFO paragraph (b) on other rate ceilings ends with its third listed circumstance and goes straight to paragraph (c) [@CL-RTE-0929].
