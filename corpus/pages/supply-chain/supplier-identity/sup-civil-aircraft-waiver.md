## Purpose

Explains the waiver of the Buy American statute for civil aircraft and related articles, which articles the provision defines as civil aircraft and related articles, and the test for when an article is a product of a country for the waiver.

## The rule

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Instrument | Codified FAR [@CL-SUP-2788] |
| Codified citation | FAR 52.225-7 [@CL-SUP-2788] |
| RFO citation | RFO FAR 52.225-7 [@CL-SUP-3628; @CL-SUP-3632; @CL-SUP-3635] |
| Edition date | Not established. |
| Text status | Final [@CL-SUP-2788] |
| Applies to | Acquisitions of civil aircraft and related articles from countries that are parties to the Agreement on Trade in Civil Aircraft [@CL-SUP-2788] |

The United States Trade Representative has waived the Buy American statute for acquisitions of civil aircraft and related articles from countries that are parties to the Agreement on Trade in Civil Aircraft [@CL-SUP-2788]. The waiver is subject to modification or withdrawal by the United States Trade Representative [@CL-SUP-2790].

Civil aircraft and related articles are all aircraft other than aircraft to be purchased for use by the Department of Defense or the United States Coast Guard [@CL-SUP-2785]. The term also covers the engines of those aircraft, together with parts and components for incorporation into the engines [@CL-SUP-2786]. It further covers any other parts, components and subassemblies for incorporation into the aircraft, and any ground flight simulators, with their parts and components, for use with respect to the aircraft [@CL-SUP-2787]. The definition applies whether the articles are to be used as original or replacement equipment in the manufacture, repair, maintenance, rebuilding, modification or conversion of the aircraft, and without regard to whether the aircraft or articles receive duty-free treatment under section 601(a)(2) of the Trade Agreements Act [@CL-SUP-2831].

For the purpose of the waiver, an article is a product of a country only if it is wholly the growth, product or manufacture of that country, or meets the alternative test [@CL-SUP-2832]. For an article that consists in whole or in part of materials from another country, the alternative test is that it has been substantially transformed into a new and different article of commerce with a name, character or use distinct from that of the article or articles from which it was transformed [@CL-SUP-2789].

## How it works

Three questions decide whether the waiver reaches an article.

First, is the article one of the defined civil aircraft and related articles? Aircraft bought for use by the Department of Defense or the United States Coast Guard are outside the definition [@CL-SUP-2785]. The engines covered are those of the aircraft in the definition, with parts and components for incorporation into the engines [@CL-SUP-2786]. Other parts, components and subassemblies, and ground flight simulators with their parts and components, are covered when they are for incorporation into the aircraft or for use with respect to the aircraft [@CL-SUP-2787]. Original or replacement use does not take an article out of the definition, and neither does the absence of duty-free treatment [@CL-SUP-2831].

Second, is the article a product of a country for the waiver? It is if it is wholly the growth, product or manufacture of that country [@CL-SUP-2832]. Otherwise it must have been substantially transformed into a new and different article of commerce with a name, character or use distinct from that of the article or articles from which it was transformed [@CL-SUP-2789].

Third, is that country a party to the Agreement on Trade in Civil Aircraft? The waiver covers acquisitions from countries that are parties to that agreement [@CL-SUP-2788]. The provision lists these countries: Albania, Austria, Belgium, Bulgaria, Canada, Croatia, Cyprus, Czech Republic, Denmark, Egypt, Estonia, Finland, France, Georgia, Germany, Greece, Hungary, Ireland, Italy, Japan, Latvia, Lithuania and Luxembourg [@CL-SUP-3031]. It also lists Macao China, Malta, Montenegro, the Netherlands, Norway, Poland, Portugal, Romania, Slovakia, Slovenia, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland, Taiwan (Chinese Taipei) and the United Kingdom [@CL-SUP-3032]. For the waiver, the parties are the countries that the provision lists [@CL-SUP-3031; @CL-SUP-3032].

The waiver can change. It is subject to modification or withdrawal by the United States Trade Representative, so read the current provision before relying on it for an acquisition [@CL-SUP-2790].

Under the RFO text, DoD contracting officers use the RFO FAR part 52 text in place of the codified text from February 1, 2026 [@CL-CMP-9760] [@CL-CMP-9762]. RFO 52.225-7 keeps the definition of civil aircraft and related articles: all aircraft other than aircraft to be purchased for use by the Department of Defense or the United States Coast Guard [@CL-SUP-3628; @CL-SUP-2785], the engines of those aircraft with parts and components for incorporation into the engines [@CL-SUP-3629; @CL-SUP-2786], and any other parts, components and subassemblies for incorporation into the aircraft and any ground flight simulators with their parts and components [@CL-SUP-3630; @CL-SUP-2787]. It keeps the original or replacement equipment language and the words without regard to duty-free treatment [@CL-SUP-3631; @CL-SUP-2831]. It keeps the waiver by the U.S. Trade Representative for acquisitions from countries that are parties to the Agreement on Trade in Civil Aircraft [@CL-SUP-3632; @CL-SUP-2788], with the same country list [@CL-SUP-3633; @CL-SUP-3634; @CL-SUP-3031; @CL-SUP-3032]. It keeps the product-of-a-country test, wholly the growth, product, or manufacture of the country or substantially transformed into a new and different article of commerce [@CL-SUP-3635; @CL-SUP-3636; @CL-SUP-2832; @CL-SUP-2789], and keeps the rule that the waiver is subject to modification or withdrawal [@CL-SUP-3637; @CL-SUP-2790].

## Worked example

Synthetic example. The numbers are made up.

A civil agency buys 2 passenger aircraft, 6 spare engines for them and 1 ground flight simulator for use with the aircraft. On a separate line the Department of Defense buys 1 trainer aircraft.

The 2 passenger aircraft are not aircraft to be purchased for use by the Department of Defense or the United States Coast Guard, so they are civil aircraft. The spare engines are engines of those aircraft, and the simulator is for use with respect to them. Being spares or replacement equipment does not take the engines out of the definition. The trainer aircraft is bought for use by the Department of Defense, so it is outside the definition and this waiver does not reach it.

The engine maker is in Canada, which is on the provision's list of parties. It builds each engine from its own parts and from one turbine module bought from a supplier in a country that is not on the list. The engine is not wholly the growth, product or manufacture of the maker's country. It is a product of that country for the waiver only if it has been substantially transformed there into a new and different article of commerce with a name, character or use distinct from that of the module and the other materials. Assuming the finished engine is distinct in name, character and use, the test is met.

If the maker had only repackaged the module and sold it under the same name and use, there would be no such transformation. The module would remain a product of the country that is not on the list, and the waiver would not reach it.

## Never confuse

This waiver is a statement by the United States Trade Representative [@CL-SUP-2788]. It is not a certificate that an offeror signs.

The product-of-a-country test applies for the purpose of this waiver [@CL-SUP-2832]. Other clauses define their own end product and construction material tests, covered on [Trade Agreements clause](sup-trade-agreements-clause.md) and [Buy American clause for supplies](sup-buy-american-supplies-clause.md).

Duty-free treatment does not decide whether an article is within the definition, because the definition applies without regard to it [@CL-SUP-2831].
