## Purpose

Explains which construction materials the contractor may use under the Buy American clause for construction materials under trade agreements, the waivers and listed exceptions, how domestic status is tested, how the cost of domestic material is judged unreasonable, how a request to use foreign material is made and decided, and what Alternates I and II change.

Under the basic clause, the contractor shall use only domestic or designated country construction material in performing the contract, except as provided in paragraphs (b)(3) and (b)(4) of the clause [@CL-SUP-2791].

Under the basic clause, because the Contracting Officer has determined that the WTO GPA and the Free Trade Agreements apply to the acquisition, the Buy American restrictions are waived for designated country construction materials [@CL-SUP-2792]. The domestic content test of the Buy American statute is waived for construction material that is a COTS item [@CL-SUP-2795]. That waiver has an exception. For construction material that consists wholly or predominantly of iron or steel or a combination of both, the domestic content test is applied only to the iron and steel content of the construction material, excluding COTS fasteners [@CL-SUP-2796].

The requirement in paragraph (b)(2) does not apply to information technology that is a commercial product or to the construction materials or components that the Government lists in the clause [@CL-SUP-2797]. The list is a bracketed fill-in. The Contracting Officer lists the excepted materials or indicates none, so the list is contract-specific and this card cannot say what any contract's list holds [@CL-SUP-2797].

The Contracting Officer may add other foreign construction material to that list if the Government determines that the cost of domestic construction material would be unreasonable [@CL-SUP-2798]. Two other grounds are that applying the restriction of the Buy American statute to a particular construction material would be impracticable or inconsistent with the public interest [@CL-SUP-2853], and that the construction material is not mined, produced or manufactured in the United States in sufficient and reasonably available commercial quantities of a satisfactory quality [@CL-SUP-2854].

## How it works

Work through the clause in this order.

Permitted material. The basic clause permits designated country construction material [@CL-SUP-2792; @CL-SUP-2791] and domestic construction material [@CL-SUP-2791]. The requirement does not reach the construction materials or components that the Government lists, or information technology that is a commercial product [@CL-SUP-2797]. The clause defines designated country construction material as a WTO GPA country, FTA country, least developed country or Caribbean Basin country construction material [@CL-SUP-2873].

Domestic status. For construction material that does not consist wholly or predominantly of iron or steel or a combination of both, domestic construction material is either an unmanufactured construction material mined or produced in the United States or a construction material manufactured in the United States that meets a listed condition [@CL-SUP-2874]. One listed condition is that the cost of its components mined, produced or manufactured in the United States exceeds 60 percent of the cost of all its components [@CL-SUP-2793]. The percentage is 65 percent for items delivered in calendar years 2024 through 2028 and 75 percent for items delivered starting in calendar year 2029 [@CL-SUP-2794]. So the percentage depends on the calendar year of delivery [@CL-SUP-2793; @CL-SUP-2794]. Components of foreign origin of the same class or kind for which nonavailability determinations have been made are treated as domestic, components of unknown origin are treated as foreign, and a construction material manufactured in the United States is also domestic if it is a COTS item [@CL-SUP-2875; @CL-SUP-2874].

Under Alternate II the first sentence of that condition, which holds the 60 percent figure and its step-ups, is replaced by a sentence in which the percentage is a blank [@CL-SUP-2865; @CL-SUP-2793; @CL-SUP-2794]. The Contracting Officer inserts the percentage in the blank [@CL-SUP-3084]. The blank is a contract-specific fill-in, so the percentage that applies under Alternate II comes from the contract.

For construction material that consists wholly or predominantly of iron or steel or a combination of both, domestic construction material is construction material manufactured in the United States if the cost of foreign iron and steel constitutes less than 5 percent of the cost of all components used in such construction material [@CL-SUP-2876]. Produced in the United States means that all manufacturing processes of the iron or steel must take place in the United States, from the initial melting stage through the application of coatings, except metallurgical processes involving refinement of steel additives [@CL-SUP-2877]. The origin of the elements of the iron or steel is not relevant to whether it is domestic or foreign [@CL-SUP-3064].

Iron and steel terms. A construction material is predominantly of iron or steel or a combination of both when the cost of the iron and steel content exceeds 50 percent of the total cost of all its components [@CL-SUP-3056]. The cost of iron and steel is the cost of the iron or steel mill products, castings or forgings utilized in the manufacture of the product, plus a good faith estimate of the cost of iron or steel components excluding COTS fasteners [@CL-SUP-3057]. Steel is an alloy that includes at least 50 percent iron and between 0.02 and 2 percent carbon, and it may include other elements [@CL-SUP-3058]. Foreign iron and steel means iron or steel products not produced in the United States [@CL-SUP-3061]. The cost of foreign iron and steel includes, but is not limited to, the cost of foreign iron or steel mill products, castings or forgings utilized in the manufacture of the construction material and a good faith estimate of the cost of all foreign iron or steel components [@CL-SUP-3062], excluding COTS fasteners [@CL-SUP-3141]. Iron or steel components of unknown origin are treated as foreign, and for a construction material with multiple components the cost of all the materials used is calculated in accordance with the definition of cost of components [@CL-SUP-3063]. A fastener is a hardware device that mechanically joins or affixes two or more objects together, and examples are nuts, bolts, pins, rivets, nails, clips and screws [@CL-SUP-3055].

Cost of components. For components purchased by the Contractor, the cost of components is the acquisition cost, including transportation costs to the place of incorporation into the construction material (whether or not paid to a domestic firm), and any applicable duty (whether or not a duty-free entry certificate is issued) [@CL-SUP-2868]. For components manufactured by the Contractor, it is all costs associated with the manufacture of the component, including transportation costs as described for purchased components, plus allocable overhead costs, but excluding profit [@CL-SUP-2869]. Cost of components does not include any costs associated with the manufacture of the construction material [@CL-SUP-2870].

COTS item. A COTS item is any item of supply, including construction material, that is a commercial product as defined in paragraph (1) of the FAR 2.101 definition, that is sold in substantial quantities in the commercial marketplace, and that meets a further condition stated next [@CL-SUP-2866]. The further condition is that it is offered to the Government, under a contract or subcontract at any tier, without modification, in the same form in which it is sold in the commercial marketplace, and a COTS item does not include bulk cargo [@CL-SUP-2867]. The COTS waiver removes the domestic content test for such an item [@CL-SUP-2795], except that iron or steel construction material is still tested on its iron and steel content [@CL-SUP-2796].

Terms. Construction material is an article, material or supply brought to the construction site by the contractor or a subcontractor for incorporation into the building or work, and includes an item brought to the site preassembled from articles, materials or supplies [@CL-SUP-3050]. Emergency life safety systems, such as emergency lighting, fire alarm and audio evacuation systems, that are discrete systems incorporated into a public building or work and are produced as complete systems [@CL-SUP-3051] are evaluated as a single and distinct construction material regardless of when or how their individual parts or components are delivered to the construction site [@CL-SUP-3052]. Materials purchased directly by the Government are supplies, not construction material [@CL-SUP-3053]. A component is an article, material or supply incorporated directly into a construction material [@CL-SUP-3054]. Foreign construction material is a construction material other than a domestic construction material [@CL-SUP-3060]. The United States means the 50 States, the District of Columbia and outlying areas [@CL-SUP-3059].

Designated country construction material. Designated country construction material is a WTO GPA country, FTA country, least developed country or Caribbean Basin country construction material [@CL-SUP-2873]. A WTO GPA country construction material includes a construction material that is wholly the growth, product or manufacture of a WTO GPA country [@CL-SUP-3065], and one that consists in whole or in part of materials from another country and has been substantially transformed in a WTO GPA country into a new and different construction material distinct from the materials from which it was transformed [@CL-SUP-3066]. The same two tests apply for a Free Trade Agreement country [@CL-SUP-3067; @CL-SUP-3068], a least developed country [@CL-SUP-3069; @CL-SUP-3070] and a Caribbean Basin country [@CL-SUP-3071; @CL-SUP-3072]. The designated countries are as follows. The WTO GPA countries include Armenia, Aruba, Australia, Austria, Belgium, Bulgaria, Canada, Croatia, Cyprus, Czech Republic, Denmark, Estonia, Finland, France, Germany and Greece [@CL-SUP-3073], Hong Kong, Hungary, Iceland, Ireland, Israel, Italy, Japan, Korea (Republic of), Latvia, Liechtenstein, Lithuania, Luxembourg, Malta, Moldova, Montenegro, Netherlands, New Zealand, North Macedonia, Norway, Poland, Portugal and Romania [@CL-SUP-3074], and Singapore, Slovak Republic, Slovenia, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland, Taiwan, Ukraine and United Kingdom [@CL-SUP-3075]. The Free Trade Agreement countries are Australia, Bahrain, Chile, Colombia, Costa Rica, Dominican Republic, El Salvador, Guatemala, Honduras, Korea (Republic of), Mexico, Morocco, Nicaragua, Oman, Panama, Peru and Singapore [@CL-SUP-3076]. The least developed countries include Afghanistan, Angola, Bangladesh, Benin, Bhutan, Burkina Faso, Burundi, Cambodia, Central African Republic, Chad, Comoros, Democratic Republic of Congo, Djibouti, Equatorial Guinea, Eritrea, Ethiopia and Gambia [@CL-SUP-3077], Guinea, Guinea-Bissau, Haiti, Kiribati, Laos, Lesotho, Liberia, Madagascar, Malawi, Mali, Mauritania, Mozambique, Nepal, Niger, Rwanda, Samoa, Sao Tome and Principe, Senegal, Sierra Leone, Solomon Islands and Somalia [@CL-SUP-3078], and South Sudan, Tanzania, Timor-Leste, Togo, Tuvalu, Uganda, Vanuatu, Yemen and Zambia [@CL-SUP-3079]. The Caribbean Basin countries include Antigua and Barbuda, Aruba, Bahamas, Barbados, Belize, Bonaire, British Virgin Islands, Curacao, Dominica, Grenada, Guyana, Haiti and Jamaica [@CL-SUP-3080], and Montserrat, Saba, St. Kitts and Nevis, St. Lucia, St. Vincent and the Grenadines, Sint Eustatius, Sint Maarten and Trinidad and Tobago [@CL-SUP-3081].

Unreasonable cost. Two paragraphs cover it, and which one applies depends on whether the material is a critical item or contains critical components. A critical item is a domestic construction material or domestic end product that is deemed critical to United States supply chain resiliency, and the list of critical items is at FAR 25.105 [@CL-SUP-2872]. A critical component is a component that is mined, produced or manufactured in the United States and deemed critical to the United States supply chain, and the list of critical components is at FAR 25.105 [@CL-SUP-2871].

For domestic construction material that is not a critical item or does not contain critical components [@CL-SUP-2842], the cost of a particular domestic construction material subject to the restrictions of the Buy American statute is unreasonable when the cost of such material exceeds the cost of foreign material by more than 20 percent [@CL-SUP-2843]. For construction material that is not a COTS item and does not consist wholly or predominantly of iron or steel or a combination of both, a further rule applies where the cost of a particular domestic construction material is determined to be unreasonable or there is no domestic offer received [@CL-SUP-2844]. Where the low offer is for foreign construction material that does not exceed 55 percent domestic content, the Contracting Officer will treat the lowest offer of foreign construction material that is manufactured in the United States and exceeds 55 percent domestic content as a domestic offer [@CL-SUP-2845]. The Contracting Officer then determines whether the cost of that offer is unreasonable by applying the evaluation factor listed in paragraph (b)(4)(i)(A)(1) of the clause [@CL-SUP-2846]. The procedures in paragraph (b)(4)(i)(A)(2) of the clause no longer apply as of January 1, 2030 [@CL-SUP-2847].

A domestic construction material that is a critical item or contains critical components is unreasonable in cost when it exceeds the cost of foreign material by more than 20 percent plus the additional preference factor identified for the critical item [@CL-SUP-2848]. For construction material that does not consist wholly or predominantly of iron or steel or a combination of both, a further rule applies where the cost of a particular domestic construction material is determined to be unreasonable or there is no domestic offer received [@CL-SUP-2849]. Where the low offer is for foreign construction material that does not exceed 55 percent domestic content, the Contracting Officer will treat the lowest offer of foreign construction material that is manufactured in the United States and exceeds 55 percent domestic content as a domestic offer [@CL-SUP-2850]. The Contracting Officer then determines whether the cost of that offer is unreasonable by applying the evaluation factor listed in paragraph (b)(4)(i)(B)(1) of the clause [@CL-SUP-2851]. The procedures in paragraph (b)(4)(i)(B)(2) of the clause no longer apply as of January 1, 2030 [@CL-SUP-2852].

The request. A contractor request to use foreign construction material under paragraph (b)(4) shall include adequate information for Government evaluation, including a description of the foreign and domestic construction materials and the unit of measure [@CL-SUP-2855]. The request shall also include the quantity, the price, the time of delivery or availability, the location of the construction project, the name and address of the proposed supplier, and a detailed justification of the reason for use of foreign construction materials cited in accordance with paragraph (b)(3) of the clause [@CL-SUP-2856]. A request based on unreasonable cost shall include a reasonable survey of the market and a completed price comparison table in the format in paragraph (d) of the clause [@CL-SUP-2799]. The price of construction material shall include all delivery costs to the construction site and any applicable duty, whether or not a duty-free certificate may be issued [@CL-SUP-2857]. To permit evaluation of a request based on unreasonable cost, the contractor shall include the information in the data format and any applicable supporting data based on the survey of suppliers [@CL-SUP-3085]. The data format is a price comparison table with columns for construction material description, unit of measure, quantity and price in dollars, and rows for foreign and domestic construction material for each item [@CL-SUP-3086]. The table's price note says the price includes all delivery costs to the construction site and any applicable duty, whether or not a duty-free entry certificate is issued [@CL-SUP-3087]. The data format also calls for the name, address, telephone number and contact of each supplier surveyed, with a copy or summary of its response attached, and other applicable supporting information [@CL-SUP-3088]. Those bracketed entries are filled in by the contractor for each request and are contract-specific.

Timing and outcome. Any contractor request for a determination submitted after contract award shall explain why the contractor could not reasonably foresee the need for the determination and could not have requested it before contract award [@CL-SUP-2858]. If the contractor does not submit a satisfactory explanation, the Contracting Officer need not make a determination [@CL-SUP-2859]. If the Government determines after contract award that an exception to the Buy American statute applies and the Contracting Officer and the Contractor negotiate adequate consideration, the Contracting Officer will modify the contract to allow use of the foreign construction material [@CL-SUP-2860]. When the basis for the exception is the unreasonable price of a domestic construction material, adequate consideration is not less than the differential established in paragraph (b)(4)(i) of the clause [@CL-SUP-2800]. Unless the Government determines that an exception to the Buy American statute applies, use of foreign construction material is noncompliant with the Buy American statute [@CL-SUP-2861].

Alternate I. Under Alternate I, the Buy American statute restrictions are waived for designated country construction materials other than Bahraini, Mexican, or Omani construction materials [@CL-SUP-2863]. Alternate I adds a definition under which Bahraini, Mexican, or Omani construction material includes construction material that is wholly the growth, product or manufacture of Bahrain, Mexico or Oman [@CL-SUP-2862]. Under Alternate I, the contractor shall use only domestic or designated country construction material other than Bahraini, Mexican, or Omani construction material in performing the contract, except as provided in paragraphs (b)(3) and (b)(4) of the clause [@CL-SUP-2864]. Alternate I also adds that Bahraini, Mexican, or Omani construction material includes a construction material that consists in whole or in part of materials from another country and has been substantially transformed in Bahrain, Mexico or Oman into a new and different construction material distinct from the materials from which it was transformed [@CL-SUP-3083]. Under Alternate I the Contracting Officer has determined that the WTO GPA and all the Free Trade Agreements except the Bahrain FTA, the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement and the Oman FTA apply to the acquisition [@CL-SUP-3082]. Bahraini, Mexican, or Omani construction material is therefore neither waived nor permitted as designated country material under Alternate I, and using it needs an exception under paragraph (b)(3) or (b)(4) [@CL-SUP-2863; @CL-SUP-2864].

Under the RFO text, DoD contracting officers use the RFO FAR part 52 text in place of the codified text from February 1, 2026 [@CL-CMP-9760] [@CL-CMP-9762]. RFO FAR 52.225-11 keeps the rule that the contractor shall use only domestic or designated country construction material in performing the contract, except as provided in paragraphs (b)(3) and (b)(4) of the clause [@CL-SUP-3588; @CL-SUP-2791]. It keeps the waiver of the Buy American restrictions for designated country construction materials because the Contracting Officer has determined that the WTO GPA and the Free Trade Agreements apply to the acquisition [@CL-SUP-3589; @CL-SUP-2792]. For a COTS item, the RFO text says a waiver applies, except that for construction material that consists wholly or predominantly of iron or steel or a combination of both, the domestic content test is applied only to the iron and steel content, excluding COTS fasteners [@CL-SUP-3590; @CL-SUP-2795; @CL-SUP-2796]. The codified sentence is followed by a cross-reference to FAR 12.505(a)(2) [@CL-SUP-9365], where the RFO sentence is followed directly by the sentence beginning In addition [@CL-SUP-9367]. It keeps the exception for information technology that is a commercial product and for the construction materials or components listed by the Government in the clause [@CL-SUP-3591; @CL-SUP-2797]. For domestic status, it keeps the 60 percent component cost condition with its 65 percent and 75 percent steps [@CL-SUP-3606; @CL-SUP-2793; @CL-SUP-2794] and the test of foreign iron and steel cost of less than 5 percent for construction material that consists wholly or predominantly of iron or steel or a combination of both [@CL-SUP-3607; @CL-SUP-2876].

It keeps the grounds on which the Contracting Officer may add foreign construction material to the list: that the cost of domestic construction material would be unreasonable [@CL-SUP-3592; @CL-SUP-2798], that applying the Buy American statute restriction to a particular construction material would be impracticable or inconsistent with the public interest [@CL-SUP-3593; @CL-SUP-2853], and that the construction material is not mined, produced, or manufactured in the United States in sufficient and reasonably available commercial quantities of a satisfactory quality [@CL-SUP-3594; @CL-SUP-2854]. It keeps the 20 percent unreasonable-cost test [@CL-SUP-3595; @CL-SUP-2843] and, for a critical item or material containing critical components, the test of 20 percent plus the additional preference factor [@CL-SUP-3597; @CL-SUP-2848]. The RFO text differs on the 55 percent domestic content rule: it prints [Reserved] for subparagraphs (b)(4)(i)(A)(2) and (A)(3) [@CL-SUP-2845; @CL-SUP-3596] and for (b)(4)(i)(B)(2) and (B)(3) [@CL-SUP-3598]. The codified subparagraphs (A)(2) and (B)(2) apply a further rule where the cost of a particular domestic construction material is determined to be unreasonable or there is no domestic offer received [@CL-SUP-2844; @CL-SUP-2849], and where the low offer is for foreign construction material that does not exceed 55 percent domestic content, the Contracting Officer treats the lowest offer of foreign construction material that is manufactured in the United States and exceeds 55 percent domestic content as a domestic offer [@CL-SUP-2845; @CL-SUP-2850]. The codified subparagraphs (A)(3) and (B)(3) say the procedures in (A)(2) and (B)(2) no longer apply as of January 1, 2030 [@CL-SUP-2847; @CL-SUP-2852].

For requests, it keeps the information the request shall include [@CL-SUP-3599; @CL-SUP-2855; @CL-SUP-3600; @CL-SUP-2856], the market survey and price comparison table for a request based on unreasonable cost [@CL-SUP-3601; @CL-SUP-2799], the explanation required for a request submitted after contract award [@CL-SUP-3602; @CL-SUP-2858], the rule that the Contracting Officer need not make a determination without a satisfactory explanation [@CL-SUP-3603; @CL-SUP-2859], the modification of the contract when adequate consideration is negotiated after an exception is determined to apply [@CL-SUP-3604; @CL-SUP-2860], and the rule that adequate consideration for an unreasonable-price exception is not less than the differential established in paragraph (b)(4)(i) [@CL-SUP-3605; @CL-SUP-2800].

## Worked example

Synthetic example. The numbers are made up.

A construction contract uses this clause with no alternate. The WTO GPA and the Free Trade Agreements apply, and the Government's list in paragraph (b)(3) says none. Four materials are delivered in 2027. None of them is a critical item.

Window units. The windows are assembled in the Republic of Korea from glass and aluminum extrusions imported from countries that are not designated. Korea (Republic of) is on the WTO GPA list. The assembly has substantially transformed the imported materials into a new and different construction material distinct from them, so the windows are WTO GPA country construction material and so designated country construction material. The Buy American restrictions are waived for them, so the contractor may use them. Under Alternate I, the same windows made in Mexico would be Mexican construction material, which is neither waived nor permitted as designated country material, so they would need an exception.

Light fixtures. The fixtures are manufactured in the United States and are a COTS item that does not consist of iron or steel. The domestic content test is waived for them, so no component percentage is computed and they are domestic construction material.

Steel door frames. The frames are a COTS item. Steel makes up 55 percent of the cost of all their components, which exceeds 50 percent, so they are predominantly of iron or steel. The waiver has its iron and steel exception, so the domestic content test is applied to the iron and steel content. The components cost $10,000 in all, and the foreign iron and steel in them costs $400, which is 4 percent. The frames contain no fasteners, so the exclusion for COTS fasteners does not come into play. The frames are manufactured in the United States, and 4 percent is less than 5 percent, so they are domestic construction material. Had the foreign iron and steel cost $600, which is 6 percent, they would not be.

Wall panels. The panels are not a COTS item and are not iron or steel. Panel supplier P manufactures panels in the United States. The cost of P's components mined, produced or manufactured in the United States is $62,000 of $100,000 for all components, which is 62 percent. Delivery is in 2027, so the percentage is 65 percent, and 62 percent does not exceed it. P's panels are not domestic construction material, although they would have passed at the 60 percent figure.

Had the contract used Alternate II with the Contracting Officer inserting 55 percent in the blank, P's 62 percent would exceed it and P's panels would be domestic construction material.

The contractor wants to use panels made abroad. Their price is $185,000, plus $9,000 for delivery to the site and $6,000 of duty, so the price used for comparison is $200,000. The contractor's request includes a price comparison table with one item, wall panels, in square meters, quantity 1,000, with a foreign price of $200,000 and a domestic price of $230,000, and it attaches the name, address, telephone number and contact of each supplier surveyed with a summary of each response. These foreign panels have 40 percent domestic content, which does not exceed 55 percent, and they are the low offer. P's panels are priced at $230,000. No offer is a domestic offer. The Contracting Officer treats P's offer, the lowest offer of foreign construction material that is manufactured in the United States and exceeds 55 percent domestic content, as a domestic offer. The Contracting Officer then applies the 20 percent evaluation factor. P's $230,000 exceeds the foreign $200,000 by $30,000, which is 15 percent. That is not more than 20 percent, so the cost is not unreasonable and the contractor cannot rely on unreasonable cost to use the foreign panels. Had P priced at $250,000, the excess would be $50,000, or 25 percent, and the cost would be unreasonable. The 55 percent procedure is available for this determination because the determination falls before January 1, 2030, when the paragraph (b)(4)(i)(A)(2) procedure stops applying. That 55 percent step follows the codified clause; the RFO text prints [Reserved] in place of the codified subparagraphs that hold it, so it prints no such step in those paragraphs [@CL-SUP-3596; @CL-SUP-3598].

Had the panels been a critical item with an additional preference factor of 10 percent, the test would be 20 percent plus 10 percent, or 30 percent. P's 15 percent excess would still not exceed it.

If the contractor asked for the foreign panels only after award, it would first have to explain why it could not reasonably foresee the need and could not have asked before award. If the Government then found an exception applies and the parties negotiated adequate consideration, the Contracting Officer would modify the contract. For an exception based on unreasonable price, the consideration would not be less than the differential established in paragraph (b)(4)(i).

## Never confuse

Several percentages in this clause test different things. The 60 percent figure, with its 65 and 75 percent step-ups, is a component cost test for manufactured material that is not mostly iron or steel [@CL-SUP-2793; @CL-SUP-2794]. The 5 percent figure is a limit on foreign iron and steel cost [@CL-SUP-2876]. The 20 percent figure is the unreasonable-cost test, with an added preference factor for critical items [@CL-SUP-2843; @CL-SUP-2848]. The 55 percent figure is a domestic content threshold used in the further rule for treating an offer as a domestic offer [@CL-SUP-2845]. The 50 percent figure is the cost threshold above which the iron and steel content makes a material predominantly of iron or steel [@CL-SUP-3056].

The COTS waiver and its iron and steel exception work together. A COTS item is waived from the domestic content test [@CL-SUP-2795], except that iron and steel construction material is still tested on its iron and steel content [@CL-SUP-2796].

The basic clause waives the Buy American restrictions for designated country construction materials because the trade agreements apply [@CL-SUP-2792]. The plain construction materials clause is a separate clause, covered on [Buy American, construction materials](sup-buy-american-construction-materials.md). The required use of American iron, steel and manufactured goods is a different clause, covered on [American iron, steel and manufactured goods](sup-american-iron-steel-manufactured-goods.md). The offeror's notice provision is covered on [Notice of Buy American requirement, construction materials under trade agreements](sup-notice-buy-american-construction-trade-agreements.md).

The codified clause has a further rule, for the case where the cost of a particular domestic construction material is determined to be unreasonable or there is no domestic offer received [@CL-SUP-2844; @CL-SUP-2849], under which the Contracting Officer, where the low offer is for foreign construction material that does not exceed 55 percent domestic content, treats the lowest offer of foreign construction material that is manufactured in the United States and exceeds 55 percent domestic content as a domestic offer [@CL-SUP-2845; @CL-SUP-2850], with procedures that no longer apply as of January 1, 2030 [@CL-SUP-2847; @CL-SUP-2852]; the RFO text prints [Reserved] for the subparagraphs that hold that rule [@CL-SUP-3596; @CL-SUP-3598].
