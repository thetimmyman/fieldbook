## Purpose

Shows which construction material the Contractor may use under the basic Balance of Payments Program clause for construction material under trade agreements, how designated country construction material is treated, the domestic content test, and what the clause says about exceptions. The alternates are on [their own card](sup-dfars-bop-construction-material-trade-agreements-alternates.md).

## The rule

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Instrument | Codified DFARS [@CL-SUP-1885] |
| Codified citation | DFARS 252.225-7045 [@CL-SUP-1885] |
| RFO citation | Not established. |
| Edition date | Not established. |
| Text status | Final [@CL-SUP-1885] |
| Applies to | Contracts with the basic trade agreements construction material clause, which waives the Balance of Payments Program restrictions for designated country construction materials [@CL-SUP-1885] |

The Contracting Officer has determined that the WTO GPA and Free Trade Agreements apply to the acquisition, and that the Balance of Payments Program restrictions are therefore waived for designated country construction materials [@CL-SUP-1885]. The Contractor shall use only domestic or designated country construction material in performing this contract, except for the listed exceptions, which include construction material valued at or below the simplified acquisition threshold in part 2 of the Federal Acquisition Regulation and information technology that is a commercial product [@CL-SUP-1886]. A further exception covers the construction material or components listed by the Government in the clause, where the Contracting Officer lists the excepted materials or indicates none [@CL-SUP-1887].

Designated country construction material means a construction material that is a WTO GPA country construction material, a Free Trade Agreement country construction material, a least developed country construction material, or a Caribbean Basin country construction material [@CL-SUP-1888]. A Free Trade Agreement country construction material is a construction material that is wholly the growth, product, or manufacture of a Free Trade Agreement country or that meets the second condition of the definition [@CL-SUP-1954]. The second condition covers a construction material that consists in whole or in part of materials from another country and has been substantially transformed in a Free Trade Agreement country into a new and different construction material distinct from the material from which it was transformed [@CL-SUP-1955]. One listed group of designated countries is the Free Trade Agreement countries, namely Australia, Bahrain, Chile, Colombia, Costa Rica, Dominican Republic, El Salvador, Guatemala, Honduras, Korea (Republic of), Mexico, Morocco, Nicaragua, Panama, Peru, or Singapore [@CL-SUP-1889].

## How it works

The exceptions are alternatives: construction material valued at or below the simplified acquisition threshold, information technology that is a commercial product, or the materials the Government lists in the clause [@CL-SUP-1886; @CL-SUP-1887].

### What counts as domestic

For construction material that does not consist wholly or predominantly of iron or steel or a combination of both, domestic construction material means either an unmanufactured construction material mined or produced in the United States [@CL-SUP-1917] or, as the second alternative, a construction material manufactured in the United States if one of two conditions is met [@CL-SUP-3989]. The first condition is that the cost of its components mined, produced, or manufactured in the United States exceeds 60 percent of the cost of all its components, except that the percentage will be 65 percent for items delivered in calendar years 2024 through 2028 and 75 percent for items delivered starting in calendar year 2029 [@CL-SUP-1918], unless an alternate percentage is established for a contract in accordance with FAR 25.201(c) [@CL-SUP-2115]. The same domestic definition and percentages appear in the domestic-only clause, covered on [the construction material clause card](sup-dfars-bop-construction-material-clause.md) [@CL-SUP-1917; @CL-SUP-1918; @CL-SUP-1914; @CL-SUP-1879]. Under that condition, components of foreign origin of the same class or kind for which nonavailability determinations have been made are treated as domestic and components of unknown origin are treated as foreign [@CL-SUP-1953]. The second condition is that the construction material is a COTS item [@CL-SUP-1953]. The cost of components is the acquisition cost, including transportation costs to the place of incorporation into the end product (whether or not such costs are paid to a domestic firm) and any applicable duty (whether or not a duty-free entry certificate is issued), for components the Contractor purchases [@CL-SUP-2111], and all costs of manufacture, including transportation costs, plus allocable overhead costs but excluding profit, for components the Contractor manufactures [@CL-SUP-2112]. The cost of components does not include any costs associated with the manufacture of the construction material [@CL-SUP-2113]. This definition reads the same as in the domestic-only clause [@CL-SUP-1940; @CL-SUP-1941; @CL-SUP-1942].

For construction material that consists wholly or predominantly of iron or steel or a combination of both [@CL-SUP-3990], domestic construction material means a construction material manufactured in the United States if the cost of iron and steel not produced in the United States (excluding fasteners), as estimated in good faith by the contractor, constitutes less than 5 percent of the cost of all the components used in such construction material [@CL-SUP-1950]. Iron or steel is produced in the United States only if all its manufacturing processes take place in the United States, except metallurgical processes involving refinement of steel additives [@CL-SUP-1951]. The cost of iron and steel not produced in the United States includes, but is not limited to, the cost of iron or steel mill products, castings or forgings not produced in the United States that are used in the manufacture of the construction material [@CL-SUP-1952]. Predominantly of iron or steel or a combination of both means that the cost of the iron and steel content exceeds 50 percent of the total cost of all its components [@CL-SUP-2114]. This definition reads the same as in the domestic-only clause [@CL-SUP-1960].

## Worked example

Synthetic example. The numbers are made up.

A contractor buys wall panels that are wholly the product of a Free Trade Agreement country, or were substantially transformed there into a new and different construction material. The panels are designated country construction material, so the contractor may use them even though they are not domestic. The contractor also buys wiring from a country that is not designated. Unless an exception applies, the wiring may not be used. Assume the wiring is not commercial information technology and the Contracting Officer listed no excepted materials.

## Never confuse

Designated country construction material is not limited to Free Trade Agreement country construction material; it also covers WTO GPA country, least developed country and Caribbean Basin country construction material [@CL-SUP-1888]. The domestic-only clause, covered on [its own card](sup-dfars-bop-construction-material-clause.md), requires only domestic construction material, except for the listed exceptions [@CL-SUP-1877]. For the FAR clause for construction materials, see [Buy American for construction materials](sup-buy-american-construction-materials.md). For the trade agreements clause that applies to end products rather than construction material, see [the trade agreements clause and certificate](sup-dfars-trade-agreements-clause-and-certificate.md).
